## WBS alignment notes
- **Post 1** (Plutarch's kyphi) maps directly to WBS topic 1. Source is excellent: both the 16-ingredient list (Moralia 383E–384C) and the preceding context about resin at dawn, myrrh at midday, and kyphi at nightfall (383A–D). The passage also covers the Pythagorean analogy (kyphi as lyre for the soul), kyphi as potion and ointment, and the day/night cosmology of incense. Very rich for a carousel.
- **Post 2** replaces WBS topic 2 (Edfu version). The Edfu laboratory texts are not in the source file. However, Aetius preserves two extraordinary kyphi recipes (36-ingredient and 28-ingredient versions) that far exceed Plutarch's 16 ingredients in complexity. The 36-ingredient version (Aetius 13.116 / Book 16) includes 500 fresh roses, pine nuts, figs, dates, asphodel juice, storax, labdanum, bdellium, and many ingredients absent from Plutarch. This makes a compelling "recipe nobody knows" post, arguably more surprising than the Edfu version since it comes from a 6th-century medical text and shows kyphi still being compounded in late antiquity.
- **Post 3** (Dioscorides' kyphi) maps directly to WBS topic 3. Source covers Diosc. 1.25 well: the recipe is shorter (roughly 10 ingredients vs Plutarch's 16), gives precise measurements (half-xestes of cyperus, 12 mnas of raisins, etc.), and includes a step-by-step method (deseed raisins, pound with wine and myrrh, sieve dry ingredients, cook honey to sticky consistency, melt resin, combine, store in earthenware). Good material for contrasting with Plutarch.
- **Post 4** replaces WBS topic 4 (Chesmou). The Edfu/Dendera texts about Chesmou are not in the source file. Instead, the source contains rich material on how kyphi migrated from Egyptian temple incense to Greco-Roman pharmaceutical use. Galen's commentary on the kyphoeidēs antidotes (13.198–203) explicitly notes the Egyptian origin and explains why the same aromatics that please the gods also treat liver disease. Archigenes' kyphi-like compounds (for liver and chest complaints), Paul of Aegina's prescription for bladder ulcers, and Alexander of Tralles' version for cold liver dyscrasia together tell the story of kyphi's second life as medicine. This is a strong DEAD WORDS, LIVING SCENTS post about how a sacred word (kyphi/kyphoeidēs) carried Egyptian temple practice into Greek pharmacology.
