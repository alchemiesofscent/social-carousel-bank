## `hippocratic-fumigations`

Series: `PHARMAKON`

Conceptual spine:
In the Hippocratic gynecological corpus, perfume is not decoration. Rose perfume, Egyptian white unguent, and other scented media are working medical forms used to fumigate, soften, redirect, and anoint.

Key passages:
- `workbench/sources/hippocrates-myron-elaion-chrisma-aleima.md:46-58`
  - `De locis in homine` on foul-smelling versus fragrant applications depending on uterine direction.
- `workbench/sources/hippocrates-myron-elaion-chrisma-aleima.md:60-69`
  - `De natura muliebri` on fumigation with aromatics and `μύρον ῥόδινον`.
- `workbench/sources/hippocrates-myron-elaion-chrisma-aleima.md:82-100`
  - Dense fumigation dossier with oil and perfume as wetting agents for aromatic materials.
- `workbench/sources/hippocrates-myron-elaion-chrisma-aleima.md:160-178`
  - Therapy that explicitly coordinates ingredients “thrown into perfumes” with oils and unguents.

Allowed claims:
- Hippocratic texts use scented preparations as therapeutic media, not as ornamental afterthoughts.
- Fragrant versus foul-smelling applications are chosen on purpose for different uterine conditions.
- Rose perfume can be part of a treatment sequence that includes fumigation and intercourse.
- The corpus distinguishes among oil, perfume, ointment, and acts of anointing rather than collapsing them into one thing.

Do not infer:
- A modern gynecology claim about efficacy.
- That all Hippocratic medicine is organized around perfume.
- That these treatments were comfortable or luxurious for patients.

Overlap to avoid:
- `kyphi-medicine`
- `rose-garland-medicine`

Drafting note:
- The hook should make the category mistake visible: perfume shows up here as a medical delivery system.

## `brain-drying-theory`

Series: `PHARMAKON`

Conceptual spine:
Galen explicitly treats certain perfumes as drugs that warm the head through smell. The nose is a route of administration, not just a way to enjoy fragrance.

Key passages:
- `workbench/sources/galen-perfume-drugs.md:37-44`
  - Perfumes administered at the nostrils so that the head is warmed by smell.
- `workbench/sources/galen-perfume-drugs.md:142-153`
  - Full source-sheet restatement with forehead and nostrils treated together.
- `workbench/sources/galen-perfume-drugs.md:78-85`
  - Galen marking luxury scenting as outside medicine.

Allowed claims:
- Galen says some perfumes act medicinally through olfactory warming.
- He links nostril application to treatment of cold head complaints.
- This is a deliberately medical use of perfume, not a blurred luxury practice.
- Galen cares about route, composition, and purpose.

Do not infer:
- A total Galenic theory of the brain from this passage alone.
- That every pleasant smell is medicinal for Galen.
- That Galen endorses luxury perfumery.

Overlap to avoid:
- `cephalic-ointment`
- `perfume-and-soul`

Drafting note:
- Lead with the route of action: he wants the smell to heat the head.

## `scent-diagnosis`

Series: `PHARMAKON`

Conceptual spine:
Ancient physicians did treat smell as clinical evidence, but not in a simple “good smell equals good medicine” way. Odor can mark crisis, rot, or pathology, yet Galen warns that smell alone does not tell you a drug’s real power.

Key passages:
- `workbench/sources/hippocrates-myron-elaion-chrisma-aleima.md:34-44`
  - `Epidemics` case where a scented application is part of a decisive clinical event.
- `workbench/sources/Rufus.md:17-18`
  - Bladder ulcers smell worst when they are rotting.
- `workbench/sources/galen-perfume-drugs.md:97-103`
  - Galen insists that powers should be judged by effects on healthy and diseased bodies, not by smell or color alone.

Allowed claims:
- Smell can function as a clinical sign in ancient medicine.
- Putrid odor is treated as evidence of serious pathology in Rufus.
- Galen explicitly refuses to reduce pharmacology to pleasant or unpleasant smell.
- The post can stage a tension between smelling as evidence and smell not being enough.

Do not infer:
- A fully developed ancient diagnostic specialty based on smell alone.
- That Galen rejects smell in practice altogether.
- That the Hippocratic case proves a stable doctrine.

Overlap to avoid:
- `brain-drying-theory`
- `rose-perfume-bladder`

Drafting note:
- Keep this carousel on clinical reading of odor, not on “ancient doctors had superhuman noses.”

## `cephalic-ointment`

Series: `PHARMAKON`

Conceptual spine:
Perfume could be medicine for the head in more than one way: Dioscorides gives rose oil as an early headache application, while Galen uses perfumed nostril and forehead anointing to reach the head through smell.

Key passages:
- `workbench/sources/dioscorides-1.43-1.63-recipes.txt:1`
  - `ῥόδινον` as cooling, useful as `κεφαλαλγίας ἔμβρεγμα ἐν ἀρχῇ`.
- `workbench/sources/galen-perfume-drugs.md:39-44`
  - Nostrils anointed so the head is warmed by smell.
- `workbench/sources/galen-perfume-drugs.md:146-153`
  - Forehead and nostril pores treated with iris, amarakinon, and nard perfumes.

Allowed claims:
- Rose oil appears in Dioscorides as an early-stage headache treatment.
- Galen uses perfumed applications on forehead and nostrils to affect the head.
- Ancient medical texts treat the head as reachable by aromatic topical and olfactory routes.

Do not infer:
- A single standardized “cephalic ointment” recipe from these passages alone.
- A modern neurological mechanism.
- That all head complaints were treated with perfume.

Overlap to avoid:
- `brain-drying-theory`
- `rose-oil-anti-inflammatory`

Drafting note:
- The hook should foreground the surprise that headache medicine can look like perfumery.

## `rose-oil-anti-inflammatory`

Series: `PHARMAKON`

Conceptual spine:
Rose oil matters medically because it is mild, cooling, and usable across inflamed or overheated conditions. Galen treats it as a real drug close to neutral, not just a nice-smelling base.

Key passages:
- `workbench/sources/dioscorides-1.43-1.63-recipes.txt:1`
  - Rose oil described as cooling, useful in compresses, wound care, headache treatment, and uterine irrigation.
- `workbench/sources/galen-perfume-drugs.md:93-103`
  - Full rose-oil passage on testing bodily effects and finding it more cooling than heating.
- `workbench/sources/galen-perfume-drugs.md:155-165`
  - Galen's definition of “good” rose oil, free from preservative additives meant only to prolong fragrance.

Allowed claims:
- Galen classifies rose oil by bodily effect rather than by smell.
- He treats it as mildly cooling and close to the neutral mixture.
- Dioscorides presents it as practically useful in multiple inflammatory or irritated conditions.
- Purity matters because preservative additives can distort the medicine.

Do not infer:
- A modern biochemical anti-inflammatory mechanism.
- That every perfumed rose preparation is equally medicinal.
- That Galen thinks stronger fragrance means stronger efficacy.

Overlap to avoid:
- `rhodinon-recipe`
- `cephalic-ointment`

Drafting note:
- Keep the claim disciplined: “one that works” means “one ancient physicians treat as reliably useful,” not modern proof language.

## `epidemic-air`

Series: `PHARMAKON`

Conceptual spine:
The strongest supportable epidemic post is not a sweeping plague history. It is the narrower ancient idea that fragrant smoke and kyphi can thin, clear, and protect the air around sick or vulnerable bodies.

Key passages:
- `workbench/kyphi.md:142-154`
  - Plutarch on physicians treating plague by rarefying air with fragrant woods.
- `workbench/sources/aet_paul.md:1210-1219`
  - Kyphi smelled or inhaled to digest catarrhs, clear the brain, and guard against epidemic conditions.
- `workbench/kyphi.md:887-887`
  - Parallel kyphi wording preserved in the workbench file.

Allowed claims:
- Ancient medical and para-medical writers connect fragrant smoke with air management in epidemic conditions.
- Plutarch explicitly links physicians, fire, and fragrant woods in plague treatment.
- Aetius/Paul present kyphi as inhaled protection for the head and against `λοιμικὰ` conditions.
- The post can frame this as environmental medicine rather than as a perfume-beauty post.

Do not infer:
- Germ theory.
- A continuous doctrine “from Hippocrates to late antiquity.”
- That kyphi was a proven anti-plague medicine in a modern sense.

Overlap to avoid:
- `kyphi-plutarch`
- `kyphi-medicine`

Drafting note:
- Use “epidemic air” or “air management” language; avoid making the post about one named historical plague.

## `please-the-sick`

Series: `PHARMAKON`

Conceptual spine:
Aretaeus makes room for remedies that the patient can bear and even welcome. Pleasant-smelling medicine is part of bedside strategy, not a trivial luxury add-on.

Key passages:
- `workbench/sources/aretaeus.md:255-259`
  - Physician should follow patient desires where not greatly harmful; among the stomachic remedies are wormwood and nard perfume.
- `workbench/sources/aretaeus.md:225-227`
  - Rose perfume or oinanthinum with fragrant white wine in treatment for gonorrhea.
- `workbench/sources/aretaeus.md:26-30`
  - In another therapeutic setting, use a perfume that is `προσηνές` and non-irritating to the touch.

Allowed claims:
- Aretaeus explicitly tells the physician to accommodate the sick person where this does not cause major harm.
- Perfumed remedies are part of that therapeutic world, especially in stomachic and other long-course treatments.
- Pleasantness and tolerability can matter in choosing a medicine.

Do not infer:
- A full ancient doctrine of bedside manners.
- That pleasant smell is the same thing as efficacy.
- A womb-rescue post that duplicates the Hippocratic fumigation material.

Overlap to avoid:
- `hippocratic-fumigations`
- `rose-perfume-bladder`

Drafting note:
- Keep the post clinical and practical: the point is that a physician sometimes has to make medicine bearable.
