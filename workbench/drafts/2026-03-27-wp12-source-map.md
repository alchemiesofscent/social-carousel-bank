## `hidden-pyxides`

Series: `ARTS OF VENUS`

Conceptual spine:
Ovid gives beauty instruction, but he insists the labor behind beauty should stay out of sight. The lover should meet the finished face, not the cosmetic apparatus on the table.

Key passages:
- `workbench/sources/ovid-ars-am-3-selections — Ars Am. 3.197-204`
  - Teeth, washing, whitening, rouge, eyebrow filling, and crocus around the eyes.
- `workbench/sources/ovid-ars-am-3-selections — Ars Am. 3.209-234`
  - Hidden cosmetic boxes (`pyxides`), foul-smelling ingredients, and the insistence that the making should not be seen.
- `workbench/sources/ovid-ars-am-3-selections — Ars Am. 3.277-280`
  - Bad breath and visible dental disorder as social risk.

Allowed claims:
- Ovid treats cosmetic improvement as a real art.
- He says the cosmetic process should be concealed from the lover.
- Smell matters because some ingredients are effective but unpleasant in the moment of preparation.
- The post can frame beauty work as stagecraft rather than as fraud.

Do not infer:
- A reconstructable perfume recipe from this passage alone.
- A general Roman ban on visible cosmetics.
- That Ovid rejects cosmetics; he rejects badly staged cosmetics.

Overlap to avoid:
- `cleopatra-cosmetics`
- `pliny-perfume-luxury`

Drafting note:
- The hook should open with the cosmetic boxes on the table, not with “Ovid wrote about beauty.”

## `munditia-no-crime`

Series: `ARTS OF VENUS`

Conceptual spine:
The `Medicamina` proem defends cosmetic care as culture rather than shame. Ovid openly says women want scented hair, ornament, and facial treatment, and then gives compound recipes instead of pretending beauty is effortless.

Key passages:
- `workbench/sources/Medicamina_Faciei_Femineae.txt:22-40`
  - `cultus` as improvement, refinement, and art.
- `workbench/sources/Medicamina_Faciei_Femineae.txt:54-76`
  - Women want adorned bodies, jewels, and scented hair; `munditia crimina nulla merent`.
- `workbench/sources/Medicamina_Faciei_Femineae.txt:90-100`
  - Ovid rejects magical shortcuts and turns to practical ingredients and mixtures.

Allowed claims:
- Ovid explicitly defends grooming and cosmetic care.
- He names scented hair as part of the desired cultivated look.
- He contrasts cosmetic art with magical or harmful remedies.
- The post can say this poem is not just moral advice; it also moves toward actual recipe-making.

Do not infer:
- A full, tested modern skincare routine from the excerpt.
- That Ovid treats cosmetics as morally neutral in every context.
- That every ingredient listed is there for fragrance rather than effect.

Overlap to avoid:
- `hidden-pyxides`
- `cleopatra-cosmetics`

Drafting note:
- Use `munditia crimina nulla merent` as the governing line, but keep the post on scented self-fashioning, not on abstract moral defense.

## `pliny-unguenta-luxus`

Series: `ARTS OF VENUS`

Conceptual spine:
Pliny and Juvenal make the same pressure visible from different angles: unguents are luxurious because they vanish fast, circulate socially, and spill into absurd forms of display.

Key passages:
- `workbench/pliny_nh_12_13.txt:627-644`
  - Unguents as the most superfluous luxury because they expire at once; scent aimed at passersby; perfumed feet, walls, baths, camp standards, and drink.
- `workbench/sources/Juvenal.md:152-186`
  - Satire 6 on acquisitive luxury, jewels, and a wife with `plus aloes quam mellis`.
- `workbench/sources/Juvenal.md:303-307`
  - Falernian-soaked unguents in drunken luxury.

Allowed claims:
- Pliny treats unguents as especially wasteful because they die quickly and mostly benefit others.
- He explicitly links unguents to display, extravagance, and absurd over-application.
- Juvenal sharpens that critique with satire of perfumed, wealthy excess.
- The post can frame perfume as a luxury whose social meaning exceeds its bodily utility.

Do not infer:
- That Romans uniformly hated perfume.
- That Pliny rejects all aromatic use rather than elite excess.
- That Juvenal is giving neutral ethnography rather than satire.

Overlap to avoid:
- `pliny-perfume-luxury`
- `nero-poppaea-funeral`

Drafting note:
- Distinguish this from the existing Pliny luxury post by centering perishability and over-application, not luxury in general.

## `wedding-threshold`

Series: `ARTS OF VENUS`

Conceptual spine:
Catullus' wedding song makes scent part of the marriage threshold: sweet-smelling marjoram, the flammeum, an anointed groom, and the bride crossing into the new house.

Key passages:
- `workbench/sources/Catullus.md — Catull. 61.7-8`
  - Hymen crowned with sweet-smelling marjoram.
- `workbench/sources/Catullus.md — Catull. 61.115-117`
  - `flammeum` and wedding torch imagery.
- `workbench/sources/Catullus.md — Catull. 61.135-136`
  - `unguentate` groom.
- `workbench/sources/Catullus.md — Catull. 61.160-161`
  - Bride crossing the threshold.
- `workbench/pliny_nh_12_13.txt:649`
  - Campania as a major Roman zone of unguent abundance; context only, not the decisive wedding proof.

Allowed claims:
- Catullus explicitly ties scented plant material and anointed male grooming to the wedding scene.
- The post can present scent as part of the passage into marriage, not just a private cosmetic act.
- The threshold, torch, veil, and scented body can be treated as one ritual ensemble.

Do not infer:
- A single universal Roman wedding perfume custom.
- That the threshold itself is explicitly perfumed in the surviving lines.
- A named perfume formula for the ceremony.

Overlap to avoid:
- `pliny-perfume-luxury`
- `myrrha-bitter`

Drafting note:
- Keep this on ritual transition and scent, not on a generic Roman wedding explainer.

## `hera-ambrosial-oil`

Series: `ARTS OF VENUS`

Conceptual spine:
The strongest local divine-toilette scene is Hera, not Aphrodite. In `Iliad 14`, her perfumed ambrosial oil is so powerful that its vapor fills earth and sky before the dressing sequence even finishes.

Key passages:
- `workbench/sources/homer-14.xml — Il. 14.170-186`
  - Hera washes, anoints herself with perfumed ambrosial oil, braids her hair, dresses, adorns herself, and sandals her feet.
- `workbench/sources/homer-14.xml — Il. 14.173-175`
  - The oil's fragrance travels through Zeus' bronze-floored house and out to earth and sky.

Allowed claims:
- The scene explicitly links divine adornment to perfumed oil.
- Smell is not incidental; the fragrance radiates cosmically.
- The toilette sequence joins oil, hair, dress, jewelry, and sandals into one preparation scene.

Do not infer:
- That this is an Aphrodite passage.
- A stable Homeric beauty manual.
- A claim about ordinary mortal cosmetics from a divine scene alone.

Overlap to avoid:
- `perfume-and-soul`
- `rose-garland-medicine`

Drafting note:
- Normalize the WBS wording to Hera in the draft itself unless a local `Cypria` text is later added.

## `anointed-corpse`

Series: `ARTS OF VENUS`

Conceptual spine:
Epic and Pliny both show that anointing does not stop with the living. Oil and unguent belong at the corpse, the pyre, and the honor paid to the dead.

Key passages:
- `workbench/sources/homer-18.xml — Il. 18.350`
  - Patroclus' body is washed and anointed with shining oil.
- `workbench/sources/homer-23.xml — Il. 23.170-186`
  - Achilles places amphorae of honey and oil at the pyre; Aphrodite anoints Hector with rosy oil.
- `workbench/pliny_nh_12_13.txt:568`
  - Pliny says unguent had come to count as an honor paid to the dead.

Allowed claims:
- Homer explicitly places oil and anointing in funerary scenes.
- `Iliad 23` gives both pyre-side oil and Aphrodite's rosy oil on Hector.
- Pliny confirms that unguent had become part of honor to the dead in Roman discourse.

Do not infer:
- A continuous, unbroken funerary doctrine from Homer to Pliny.
- That every funerary oil was a luxury perfume.
- A generalized embalming claim.

Overlap to avoid:
- `nero-poppaea-funeral`
- `rose-garland-medicine`

Drafting note:
- Center the category surprise: perfume belongs at the corpse as well as the dressing table.
