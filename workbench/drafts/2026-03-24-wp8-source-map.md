## `rhodinon-simplicity`

Series: `ARTS OF VENUS`

Conceptual spine:
Rose perfume became the widely shared perfume partly because roses were abundant and the mixture stayed comparatively simple, even while its prestige shifted from city to city.

Existing overlap to avoid:
- `rhodinon-recipe`

Source notes:
- Dioscorides gives the recipe mechanics, repeated rose infusions, and ancillary additives. Use only as background, not as the narrative spine. `workbench/sources/dioscorides-1.43-1.63-recipes.txt:1`
- Pliny says rose perfume was the most widespread because roses grew abundantly almost everywhere, and he calls the mixture comparatively simple. `workbench/pliny_nh_12_13.txt:588`
- Athenaeus names the cities famed for the best rhodinon and notes that such reputations shifted over time. `workbench/tlg0008001.xml:1466-1468`
- Hicesius, quoted by Athenaeus, calls rhodinon suitable for drinking. `workbench/tlg0008001.xml:1502-1505`
- Theophrastus classifies rhodinon among flower-based perfumes. `workbench/sources/theophrastus-on-odors.txt:27`

Allowed claims:
- Rhodinon was broadly distributed and familiar.
- Its formula could be described as comparatively simple in relation to richer blends.
- Prestige attached to producing cities, not just ingredients.
- It belongs to the flower-perfume class.

Do not infer:
- Explicit democratic or anti-elite politics beyond what the texts support.
- Cheapness as a hard claim.
- Recipe-step recap as the main content.

## `amarakinon-false-name`

Series: `ARTS OF VENUS`

Conceptual spine:
Amarakinon’s name advertised one thing, but Theophrastus treats the best version as a prestige perfume built from the finest aromatics rather than from amaracus itself.

Existing overlap to avoid:
- `amarakinon-recipe`
- `amaracus`

Source notes:
- Dioscorides says the best amarakinon is made at Cyzicus and gives the enriched formula. `workbench/sources/dioscorides-1.43-1.63-recipes.txt:16`
- Athenaeus, quoting Apollonius, says the best amarakinon is Coan; the point is shifting prestige by place, not stable essence. `workbench/tlg0008001.xml:1469-1470`
- Athenaeus also quotes Theophrastus on perfume classes and root-derived perfumes, including amarakinon from costus. `workbench/tlg0008001.xml:1512-1521`
- Theophrastus says perfumes can be misleadingly named: the best amarakinon uses the finest aromatics, but not amaracus, because the plant’s scent is weak and fades quickly. `workbench/sources/theophrastus-on-odors.txt:30`
- Theophrastus groups amarakinon with the longer-lasting perfumes. `workbench/sources/theophrastus-on-odors.txt:38`

Allowed claims:
- The name and the ingredient list do not line up straightforwardly.
- Amarakinon is a prestige blend whose identity depends on craftsmanship and convention.
- Ancient authorities disagreed about the city that made the best version.
- It belongs with the root-derived perfumes in Theophrastus’ classification.

Do not infer:
- Bridal or funerary use from this source set.
- A second megalleion post.
- A claim that amaracus was absent from every version.

## `oinanthion-clear-head`

Series: `ARTS OF VENUS`

Conceptual spine:
Oinanthion turns grape blossom into perfume, and Greek medical-social writing presents it as a drink-friendly scent that preserves mental clarity.

Existing overlap to avoid:
- `oinanthion-recipe`

Source notes:
- Dioscorides gives the short recipe and says its force is analogous to rhodinon apart from the laxative effect. `workbench/sources/dioscorides-1.43-1.63-recipes.txt:4`
- Athenaeus lists the best oinanthion as Cypriot, then Adramyttian, and notes later improvement at Adramyttium. `workbench/tlg0008001.xml:1468-1469`
- Athenaeus on Stratonice explains the prestige shift toward Adramyttium. `workbench/tlg0008001.xml:1483-1484`
- Hicesius says oinanthion is good for drinking and keeps the mind unobstructed. `workbench/tlg0008001.xml:1505-1507`
- Theophrastus classifies oinanthion as a leaf-based perfume and says the vine blossom from Cyprus was fragrant while the Greek one was not. `workbench/sources/theophrastus-on-odors.txt:27`

Allowed claims:
- Oinanthion is tied to vine blossom rather than petals or roots.
- Cypriot material had prestige because of fragrance.
- It had a social-medical reputation as drink-suitable and clear-headed.
- Place and patronage affected prestige.

Do not infer:
- “Cheapest luxury.”
- A generic wine-perfume identity.
- Recipe recap as the main narrative.

## `krokinon-drinking`

Conceptual spine:
Krokinon mattered less as a symbolic saffron cloud than as a named perfume whose prestige moved by city and whose usable form for drinking avoided too much myrrh.

Existing overlap to avoid:
- `krokinon-recipe`

- Athenaeus preserves the same prestige geography. `workbench/tlg0008001.xml:1467-1468`

Allowed claims:
- Krokinon carried regional prestige labels.
- It had a drink-suitable version defined by balance.
- Saffron perfume was treated as a named prepared blend, not just saffron in oil.

Do not infer:

- Color-symbolism arguments.
- A full recipe retelling.

## `nardinum-distance`

Series: `DEAD WORDS, LIVING SCENTS`

Conceptual spine:
Nardinum is a perfume of distance: imported nard, many substitutes, and urban prestige all press against one another inside the same name.

Existing overlap to avoid:
- `nardinum-recipe`

Source notes:
- Dioscorides gives the fuller and simpler nardinum variants and says the good one is thin, not harsh, and smells of dry nard or amomum. `workbench/sources/dioscorides-1.43-1.63-recipes.txt:20`
- Pliny gives the composition for nardinum or foliatum. `workbench/pliny_nh_12_13.txt:606`
- Pliny immediately notes how many herbs imitated Indian nard, making adulteration or substitution a structural issue. `workbench/pliny_nh_12_13.txt:609`
- Athenaeus says the best nardinum was made at Tarsus. `workbench/tlg0008001.xml:1468`
- Athenaeus quoting Theophrastus classifies nardinum among root perfumes. `workbench/tlg0008001.xml:1519-1520`
- Theophrastus treats root perfumes as stronger and longer-lasting, and notes long persistence among some such perfumes. `workbench/sources/theophrastus-on-odors.txt:28`
- Theophrastus says amarakinon, irinum, and nardinum last longest. `workbench/sources/theophrastus-on-odors.txt:38`
- Theophrastus adds that nard and myrrh preserve scent especially well. `workbench/sources/theophrastus-on-odors.txt:56`

Allowed claims:
- Nardinum depended on distance and import prestige.
- Its name sat under pressure from substitutes and imitation.
- Root-perfume classification matters to how ancient authors imagined its staying power.
- City-based craftsmanship shaped reputation.

Do not infer:
- A full India-to-Rome trade-route narrative.
- Buyer advice already covered by recipe or marketplace posts.
- A universal claim that every nardinum was adulterated.

## `cyprinon-egypt`

Series: `DEAD WORDS, LIVING SCENTS`

Conceptual spine:
Cyprinon shows how perfume prestige moved: from Cyprus in the name, toward Egypt and Sidon in reputation, while durability and careful handling remained central to the blend.

Existing overlap to avoid:
- `kyprinon-recipe`

Source notes:
- Dioscorides gives the recipe and repeated infusion logic and says the best version smells fresh and sharp. `workbench/sources/dioscorides-1.43-1.63-recipes.txt:13`
- Theophrastus says the work on cyprus perfume is like rhodinon, but if you do not remove and press it quickly, rot ruins the perfume because the plant turns wet. `workbench/sources/theophrastus-on-odors.txt:26`
- Pliny says cyprinum first had prestige in Cyprus, then in Egypt, while Phoenicia later overtook much else. `workbench/pliny_nh_12_13.txt:576-579`
- Pliny gives the composition and says the best cyprinum came from Sidon, next Egypt; without sesame oil it lasts four years. `workbench/pliny_nh_12_13.txt:597`
- Athenaeus says Egyptian cyprinum is preferred, with Cyprus, Phoenicia, and Sidon ranked behind it. `workbench/tlg0008001.xml:1470-1474`
- Athenaeus quoting Theophrastus classifies cyprinum among flower perfumes. `workbench/tlg0008001.xml:1512-1516`

Allowed claims:
- Prestige moved geographically and the name did not freeze origin.
- Durability was part of cyprinum’s profile.
- Production demanded fast handling to avoid spoilage.
- Egypt and Sidon became important prestige centers.

Do not infer:
- “Sacred oil of Canaan.”
- A singular religious meaning for the perfume.
- A recipe-only post.

## `irinum-twenty-years`

Series: `DEAD WORDS, LIVING SCENTS`

Conceptual spine:
Irinum was memorable not because it was hardest to make, but because writers treated it as a dry, durable perfume that could astonishingly persist for decades.

Existing overlap to avoid:
- `irinum-recipe`

Source notes:
- Theophrastus says the best irinum is made when the iris stays dry and unheated. `workbench/sources/theophrastus-on-odors.txt:24`
- Theophrastus classifies irinum among root perfumes. `workbench/sources/theophrastus-on-odors.txt:28`
- Theophrastus says one perfume dealer had irinum twenty years old and amarakinon seventeen years old. `workbench/sources/theophrastus-on-odors.txt:38`
- Theophrastus later says nard and myrrh preserve scent especially well, but this section remains relevant for perfume longevity as an ancient concern. `workbench/sources/theophrastus-on-odors.txt:56`
- Pliny says irinum was first most valued at Corinth, later at Cyzicus. `workbench/pliny_nh_12_13.txt:576`
- Athenaeus says the best iris perfume was in Elis and Cyzicus. `workbench/tlg0008001.xml:1465-1466`

Allowed claims:
- Irinum had a strong durability reputation.
- The famous twenty-year anecdote is the main hook.
- Prestige shifted between cities.
- Dry handling mattered to quality.

Do not infer:
- “Most labor-intensive perfume process.”
- Storage technologies not mentioned in the sources.
- Recipe mechanics as the main narrative.
