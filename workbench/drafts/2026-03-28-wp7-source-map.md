## `double-perfumers-vessel`

Series: `TOOLS OF THE TRADE`

Conceptual spine:
The clearest workshop vessel is not a luxury bottle but a doubled perfumer's vessel built for controlled soaking and indirect heat. Ancient recipes describe it by function: stoppered, covered, bound, stirred, and only then warmed gently.

Key passages:
- `workbench/sources/tools-texts.md#term-double-vessel`
  - `δίπλωμα` as shorthand for gentle, indirect heating in Aetius 1.113.
- `workbench/sources/tools-texts.md#term-tin-lined-vessels`
  - Aetius xii.55 and Galen, *De comp. med. per genera* 7 on tin perfumer's vessels, repeated stirring, lid, coverings, and later boiling in a doubled setup.
- `workbench/sources/tools-texts.md#term-diployn-aggeion-myrepsikon`
  - Explicit phrase `διπλοῦν ἀγγεῖον μυρεψικόν`; smaller vessel set into a larger heated one.

Allowed claims:
- Ancient perfumery distinguished direct fire from protected, indirect heating.
- The doubled perfumer's vessel is a process tool, not just a storage pot.
- Some recipes specify tin or tin-treated perfumer's vessels.
- The texts describe a repeated workflow of soaking, stoppering, covering, stirring, and later warming.

Do not infer:
- One fixed vessel shape.
- That every perfumer's vessel was tin or tin-lined.
- That every doubled vessel in every passage is the same apparatus in identical dimensions.

Overlap to avoid:
- `alabastron`
- `perfumers-shade`

Drafting note:
- Lead with the category mistake: the most important perfumery vessel is not for display but for controlled heating.

## `organon-not-cloth`

Series: `TOOLS OF THE TRADE`

Conceptual spine:
When Dioscorides says bruised myrrh is squeezed `δι' ὀργάνου`, the important point is not the exact machine but the job. `Organon` is the extraction apparatus, not the cloth.

Key passages:
- `workbench/sources/tools-texts.md#term-organon`
  - Dioscorides 1.60 on stacte: myrrh bruised with a little water and squeezed through an `organon`.
- `workbench/sources/tools.md#term-organon`
  - Editorial summary distinguishing apparatus from basket or cloth.
- `workbench/sources/tools-texts.md#term-kyrtis`
  - Contrast case: `kyrtis` is the wicker basket used with pressure, not the pressing device itself.

Allowed claims:
- In the perfume passage, `organon` names the extraction apparatus.
- The term is best treated as a constructed or engineered pressing device of unclear exact form.
- `Organon` belongs with pressing language, not with covers, cloths, or storage.

Do not infer:
- A screw-press with certainty.
- That `organon` here means only a generic “tool” with no technical force.
- That the term itself tells us the apparatus' material, shape, or scale.

Overlap to avoid:
- `kyrtis-pressing-basket`
- `double-perfumers-vessel`

Drafting note:
- The hook should ride the uncertainty cleanly: we do not know exactly what it looked like, but we do know it was the thing doing the squeezing.

## `kyrtis-pressing-basket`

Series: `TOOLS OF THE TRADE`

Conceptual spine:
The `kyrtis` is not the press. It is the woven basket that holds the material while pressure does the work.

Key passages:
- `workbench/sources/tools-texts.md#term-kyrtis`
  - Dioscorides 1.49 and 1.52 on taking material up into the basket and pressing it out.
- `workbench/sources/tools-texts.md#cit-kyrtis-early-cit-2`
  - Paul 7.20.11: sesame pressed out `through wicker baskets on a screw-press`.
- `workbench/sources/tools.md#term-kyrtis`
  - Editorial distinction among basket, press, and cloth.

Allowed claims:
- `Kyrtis` is a woven basket used as a pressing filter or strainer.
- The basket can work together with stronger pressing apparatus.
- Ancient pressing had separate components: what applies force and what holds the material.

Do not infer:
- That `kyrtis` is just a cloth bag.
- That every pressing basket had the same weave, shape, or capacity.
- That every basket passage implies a screw-press.

Overlap to avoid:
- `organon-not-cloth`
- `breathing-jars`

Drafting note:
- Make the contrast visual: basket under pressure, not basket as storage.

## `holmos-and-spathe`

Series: `TOOLS OF THE TRADE`

Conceptual spine:
The workshop is not only vessels and presses. A heavy mortar and a stirring paddle show the perfumer actively breaking, wetting, mixing, and managing the batch by hand.

Key passages:
- `workbench/sources/tools-texts.md#term-holmos`
  - Dioscorides 1.54 on myrrh worked in a `holmos`.
- `workbench/sources/tools-texts.md#term-spathe`
  - Dioscorides 1.55 on stirring continuously with a `spathe` until the mixture cools.
- `workbench/sources/tools-texts.md#cit-spathe-cit-3`
  - Aetius xii.55 on stirring with a perfumer's spatula and wiping it clean.
- `workbench/sources/tools.md#term-thyia-igdion`
  - Supporting grinding pair `θυία` / `ἰγδίον` for different preparation scales.

Allowed claims:
- Ancient perfume work included grinding before infusion and repeated stirring during processing.
- `Holmos` is a working mortar, not just a generic bowl.
- `Spathe` in the recipe passages is a stirring implement, sometimes specified as palm-wood.
- Tool hygiene mattered because residues could sour and damage the batch.

Do not infer:
- A single standardized tool kit from these passages alone.
- That every occurrence of `spathe` means the stirring tool; some passages use the word differently.
- That the grinding passages let us reconstruct precise materials or dimensions for every implement.

Overlap to avoid:
- `double-perfumers-vessel`
- `scratchandsniff`

Drafting note:
- Keep the post kinetic: grind, stir, wipe, repeat.

## `breathing-jars`

Series: `TOOLS OF THE TRADE`

Conceptual spine:
Ancient perfumers did not always try to seal everything airtight. Some mixtures had to breathe through loose cloth or mat, while other setups were sealed hard with clay all the way around.

Key passages:
- `workbench/sources/tools-texts.md#term-othonion`
  - Aetius 1.114: cover the jar mouth with a loose cloth `ἵνα διαπνέηται τὸ ἔλαιον`.
- `workbench/sources/tools-texts.md#term-psiathos-araia`
  - Dioscorides 1.45: loose mat as porous barrier over the vessel.
- `workbench/sources/tools-texts.md#term-pelos`
  - Aetius 1.135: smear clay all around the vessel-joint to seal it.
- `workbench/sources/tools-texts.md#term-derma`
  - Hide added outside the linen in outdoor steeping because of sudden rain.

Allowed claims:
- Ancient perfumery used more than one closure regime.
- Loose cloths and mats could regulate exposure rather than eliminate it.
- Clay lute could create a tight seal where vapor needed to stay inside.
- Covers, breathable barriers, and full seals are different technical choices.

Do not infer:
- That every perfume jar was sealed airtight.
- That every cloth in the corpus is a strainer.
- That the sealed distillation/fumigation setup represents the normal state of all perfume work.

Overlap to avoid:
- `double-perfumers-vessel`
- `organon-not-cloth`

Drafting note:
- Use the sharp contrast: some jars had to breathe; some joints had to be sealed shut.

## `spotting-a-perfumer-shop`

Series: `TOOLS OF THE TRADE`

Conceptual spine:
The safest workshop post is not “here is the standard perfumer's room.” It is “here is how texts and archaeology let you recognize a perfumer's shop or atelier at all.”

Key passages:
- `workbench/sources/tools-texts.md#term-myrepsike`
  - `μυρεψική` as the craft of perfumery one can set up and finance.
- `workbench/sources/tools-texts.md#term-myrepsos`
  - `μυρεψός` as perfumer/compounder, distinct from the seller.
- `workbench/sources/tools-texts.md#term-myropoleion`
  - Literal perfume-shop where one goes to ask the price of cyprus-oil.
- `workbench/sources/tools-texts.md#term-myropoles`
  - Theophrastus fragment on perfume-sellers seeking shaded upper rooms.
- `workbench/sources/Pages from Brun and Fernandez - 2015 - Parfums antiques de l'archéologue au chimiste.pdf`
  - Chapter 2 on the archaeology of ancient perfumeries: workshop identification is recent and difficult; the chapter treats flacons, decorated vessels, `unguentarii`, and case-study ateliers as evidence rather than as one standard plan.

Allowed claims:
- Greek and later texts preserve distinct words for perfumers, perfume-sellers, and perfume-shops.
- Theophrastus ties the perfume trade to shaded upper rooms suited to preservation.
- Archaeological identification of perfumers' workshops is difficult and relatively recent.
- Shops and ateliers are recognized through clustered evidence, not through one universal room template.

Do not infer:
- A single standardized Mediterranean perfumer's workshop floorplan.
- That every perfume-seller was also the compounder.
- That the Brun/Fernandez chapter licenses a cinematic reconstruction of the whole room.

Overlap to avoid:
- `perfumers-shade`
- `perfume-shops`

Drafting note:
- The hook should make the evidence problem visible: a perfumer's shop is something you identify from signs, stock, and technical traces, not from one iconic blueprint.
