## Carousel 1: `horror-recipe` — PHARMAKON

**Series:** PHARMAKON

**Spine:** A Greek magical papyrus recipe combines luxury temple aromatics (myrrh, saffron, frankincense) with a field mouse, moon beetles, goat fat, baboon faeces, and ibis eggs. The combination is not accidental. Medicine and magic drew from the same pharmacopeia. The horror was part of the efficacy claim — the luxury aromatics don't redeem the recipe; they implicate it.

**Source passage (GEMF 57 = PGM IV 2455–71):**
Ingredients listed in the recipe: field-mouse made divine with spring water; two moon beetles made divine with river water; river crab; fat of a spotted virgin goat; faeces of a dog-faced baboon; two ibis' eggs; two drachms of styrax; two drachms of myrrh; two drachms of saffron; four drachms of Italian cyperus; four drachms of uncut frankincense; a single onion.

**Interpretation (Coughlin):** The recipe recruits the same luxury aromatics used in temple offerings and medical pharmacy — myrrh, frankincense, saffron — alongside ingredients that are scatologically and ritually polluting. The contrast is structural. The same ingredient that consecrates a temple appears in a recipe that also calls for baboon dung. Medicine and magic drew from the same pharmacopeia; the difference was what else you added.

## Carousel 2: `pachrates-hadrian` — PHARMAKON

**Series:** PHARMAKON

**Spine:** A high priest from Heliopolis named Pachrates staged a magical demonstration before the Emperor Hadrian. The claim: attract a woman in one hour, make her sick in two hours, kill her in seven. The paper reads this as *epideixis* — horror as public performance. The victim's body undergoing the procedure was simultaneously the medium and the proof.

**Source passage (GEMF 57 = PGM IV 2441–55):**
The papyrus prologue claims Pachrates (high priest of Heliopolis) demonstrated his procedures before Hadrian: "attracted in one hour, made sick in two hours, destroyed in seven hours." Hadrian was allegedly so convinced he doubled Pachrates' pay.

**Interpretation (Coughlin):** The demonstration structured horror as spectacle. The procedure's claim to power rested on the victim's body — a body that had to suffer, on schedule, before an audience that expected to be convinced. The horror isn't in the ritual ingredients; it's in the structure: a demonstration that required a victim.

## Carousel 3: `blood-ink` — FROM THE WORKSHOP

**Series:** FROM THE WORKSHOP
**Badge:** `✦ WORKSHOP` required on hook slide

**Spine:** The Greek magical papyri specify blood as an ingredient in certain ritual inks. Sean's experimental reconstruction tested whether blood served a practical ink function (better writability, better pigment). Result: blood does not improve the ink. Blood was in the recipe because it made the act of writing horrifying — the smell, the viscosity, the transgression. The sensory experience was the ingredient.

**Source passage (GEMF 68 = PGM XXXVI 264–74):**
"Take blood of a night owl and myrrh ink, mix the two together, and with a new reed draw the figure, as appended, on a clean piece of paper..."

**Greek from recipe:** Λαβὼν αἷμα νυκτιβαοῦτος καὶ ζμυρνομέλαν...

**Experimental reconstruction (Coughlin):**
- Tested 8 conditions: carbon ink (burnt myrrh), carbon + blood; iron-gall; iron-gall + burnt myrrh; iron-gall + raw myrrh; iron-gall + blood; iron-gall + blood + burnt myrrh; iron-gall + blood + raw myrrh
- Key finding: blood does not react with gallic acid to produce ferrous tannate (the iron-gall pigment). Reason: iron in blood is protein-bound (haemoglobin, ferritin) — not freely available as Fe(ii) ions.
- Carbon ink: blood did not darken it; blood and non-blood versions visually indistinguishable on paper
- Iron-gall inks: no visible difference between blood and non-blood versions
- Conclusion (Coughlin): "it seems unlikely that the blood is added to the ink because of its contribution to it as an ink... The superfluousness of blood in an already constituted, useable ink, suggests it is added for a magical purpose"
- Sensorial: blood was shiny, slimy, deep red, smelled of metal; did not incorporate easily into ink
