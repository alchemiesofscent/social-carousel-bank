# The Olfactory Lexicon of Dioscorides
**A Comprehensive Guide to Scent Terminology in *De Materia Medica***

For the first-century pharmacologist Dioscorides, smell was not merely an aesthetic experience; it was a primary diagnostic tool, a marker of botanical purity, and a physical vector for medicine. His vocabulary reflects a highly practical, clinical approach to olfaction. 

Below is a comprehensive categorization of the scent terminology used in his work, divided by qualitative descriptions, comparative terms, and the physical mechanics of smelling.

---

## PART 1: The Primary Scent Categories (Adjectives & Nouns)

### 1. The "Sweet" Scent (Εὐωδία / Εὐώδης)
*   **Transliteration:** *Euodia* (noun) / *Euodēs* (adjective)
*   **Translation:** Sweet smell, fragrance, good odor.
*   **Pharmacological Context:** The highest compliment for a botanical. It denotes the intrinsic, desirable fragrance of genuine spices, resins, and herbs. *Euodia* is an active property utilized for hygiene and perfumery, and it serves as the ultimate test of purity, often revealed when a substance is burned.
*   **Example (Testing pure Frankincense bark):** 
    > "ὁ μέντοι τοῦ λιβάνου φλοιὸς ἀνάπτεται καὶ **μετ' εὐωδίας** ἐκθυμιᾶται." (1.68.5)
    > *"The bark of [genuine] frankincense, however, catches fire and burns **with a sweet fragrance**."*

### 2. The "Striking" Scent (Πληκτικόν)
*   **Transliteration:** *Plēktikon* (from *plēssō*, "to strike or smite")
*   **Translation:** Striking, pungent, penetrating, arresting.
*   **Pharmacological Context:** A mark of supreme freshness and potency in aromatics. A *plēktikon* scent physically "strikes" the nose, indicating that the plant is still rich in volatile essential oils. Fake or stale goods lack this vigorous punch.
*   **Example (Selecting pure Malabathron/Indian Bay):** 
    > "[Good malabathron is] **πληκτικὸν τῇ ὀσμῇ** καὶ ἐπιδιαμένον τῇ εὐωδίᾳ." (1.12.2)
    > *"[Good malabathron is] **striking in its smell** and enduring in its fragrance."*

### 3. The "Heavy" Scent (Βαρύοσμον)
*   **Transliteration:** *Baryosmon*
*   **Translation:** Heavy-smelling, oppressive, dense.
*   **Pharmacological Context:** Used to describe the dense, oppressive scent of poisonous plants (like hemlock), narcotic herbs (like mandrake), or intensely bitter purgatives (like aloe). It is also frequently used to distinguish inferior, wild plant varieties from sweeter, cultivated ones. 
*   **Example (Describing Aloe):**
    > "**βαρύοσμος** δὲ ὅλη καὶ ἀπογευομένῳ πικροτάτη." (3.22.1)
    > *"[Aloe] is entirely **heavy-smelling** and exceedingly bitter to the taste."*

### 4. The "Stinking" Scent (Βρωμώδης)
*   **Transliteration:** *Bromodēs* (from *bromos*, a stench)
*   **Translation:** Stinking, foul-smelling, fetid, nauseating.
*   **Pharmacological Context:** Reserved for things that smell objectively bad and biological—often rotting materials, animal products (like castoreum), or harsh, nauseating minerals. It is an olfactory red flag for fraudulent or extremely cheap botanical substitutes.
*   **Example (Describing the mineral Sori):**
    > "ἔτι δὲ στῦφον καὶ πρὸς τὴν κατάποσιν ἢ ὄσφρησιν **βρωμῶδες** καὶ ἀνατρεπτικὸν στομάχου." (5.102.1)
    > *"It is astringent, and **foul-stinking** to the swallowing or smelling, and upsets the stomach."*

### 5. The "Ill" Scent (Δυσώδης / Δυσωδία)
*   **Transliteration:** *Dysodēs* (adjective) / *Dysodia* (noun)
*   **Translation:** Ill-smelling, bad odor, clinical stench.
*   **Pharmacological Context:** Dioscorides’ general clinical term for bad body odors (bad breath, sweat, rotting gums, festering wounds) that his medicines are designed to cure. It is also used to describe the acrid, unpleasant scent of raw animal fats before they are purified for perfumery.
*   **Example (Curing bad breath):**
    > "τὰς δὲ **δυσωδίας** τοῦ στόματος ἀπαλλάττει ἀγαλόχου ἀπόβρεγμα." (Eup. 1.80.1)
    > *"A decoction of agallochum relieves **bad smells** of the mouth."*

## PART 2: Comparative Aromatics 

### 6. "Smelling of [X]" (The *-ízō* Verbs)
*   **Greek Construction:** Verbs ending in -ίζω (*-ízō*) applied to smells.
*   **Pharmacological Context:** Dioscorides constantly uses these verbs to describe complex aromatic profiles by comparing an unknown, exotic, or complex plant to a familiar one.
*   **Examples:**
    *   **Nardizonta (Smelling of Nard):** "[Phu has roots that are] fragrant, **smelling of nard** (*ναρδίζοντα τῇ ὀσμῇ*)." (1.11.1)
    *   **Kastorizon (Smelling of Castoreum/Musk):** "[Onyx shells] are fragrant when burned, **smelling somewhat of castoreum** (*καστορίζοντες*)." (2.8.1)
    *   **Peganizousa (Smelling of Rue):** "[The best cinnamon has] a smell **resembling rue** (*ἡ ὀσμὴ πηγανίζουσα*)." (1.14.1)

## PART 3: The Mechanics of Scent and Olfaction

### 7. "The Action of Smelling" (Olfaction as Medicine)
*   **Greek Construction:** ὀσφραίνομαι / κατὰ ὄσφρησιν / ὀσφραντός (*osphrainomai* / *kata osphresin* / *osphrantos*)
*   **Translation:** "To smell/sniff," "by means of smelling," "able to be smelled."
*   **Pharmacological Context:** The nose is an intake valve for drugs. Smelling is an active mechanical action used to administer medicines that wake fainting patients (smelling salts) or induce narcotic sleep. It is also a command to the reader to actively sniff a drug to verify its identity.
*   **Example (Using Sagapenum as smelling salts):**
    > "διεγείρει καὶ ὑστερικῶς πνιγομένας μετ' ὄξους **ὀσφραινόμενον**." (3.81.2)
    > *"[Sagapenum] rouses women suffocating from hysterical fits **when smelled** with vinegar."*

### 8. "Filling the Sense of Smell" (Olfactory Fatigue)
*   **Greek Construction:** πληροῦντα τὴν ὄσφρησιν (*plērounta tēn osphrēsin*)
*   **Translation:** "Filling the sense of smell."
*   **Pharmacological Context:** Used to describe an overpowering, highly volatile fragrance that physically saturates the olfactory receptors, hindering the ability to smell anything else.
*   **Example (Warning against olfactory fatigue when grading Cinnamon):**
    > "τὰ κρείττονα περιπνέοντα καὶ **πληροῦντα τὴν ὄσφρησιν** ἐμποδίζει τὴν τοῦ ἥττονος ἐπίκρισιν." (1.14.2)
    > *"The better [cinnamon fragments], breathing around and **filling the sense of smell**, hinder the judgment of the lesser [quality cinnamon]."*

### 9. "Breathing / Exhaling" (The Active Scent)
*   **Greek Construction:** πνέον / περιπνέοντα (*pneon* / *peripneonta*)
*   **Translation:** "Breathing," "exhaling," or "breathing around."
*   **Pharmacological Context:** Scent is treated as an active exhalation. High-quality aromatic oils or spices actively "breathe" their essence into the surrounding air.
*   **Example (Testing pure Iris perfume):**
    > "κράτιστον δὲ ὑπάρχει τὸ ἐν τῇ ὀσφρήσει μηδενὸς ἄλλου, μόνης δὲ τῆς ἴριδος **πνέον**." (1.56.3)
    > *"The best is that which, upon smelling, **breathes** of nothing else, but only of iris."*

## PART 4: Scent as a Marker of Potency and Purity

### 10. "Full of Smell / Vigorous in Smell"
*   **Greek Construction:** πλήρης ὀσμῆς / εὔτονος τῇ ὀσμῇ (*plērēs osmēs* / *eutonos tē osmē*)
*   **Translation:** "Full of smell," "vigorous in smell."
*   **Pharmacological Context:** Instructions for selecting the best raw materials in the marketplace. A plant that is "full" or "vigorous" in its scent is fresh, unadulterated, and rich in active pharmacological properties.
*   **Example (Selecting fresh Peucedanum root):**
    > "ἐκλέγου δὲ τὴν πρόσφατον, ἄβρωτον, στερεάν, **πλήρη ὀσμῆς**." (3.78.4)
    > *"Choose that which is fresh, uneaten [by bugs], solid, and **full of smell**."*

### 11. "Casting off the Smell" (Purification)
*   **Greek Construction:** ἀποβάλλω τὴν ὀσμήν / τὴν δυσωδίαν (*apoballō tēn osmēn / tēn dysodian*)
*   **Translation:** "To cast off / lose / shed its smell / its stink."
*   **Pharmacological Context:** Used exclusively in the preparation of raw animal fats (beef, goat, goose) for perfumery. The raw fat must be boiled and washed until its intrinsic, unpleasant biological smell is completely neutralized, creating an odorless base for perfumes.
*   **Example (Purifying beef fat):**
    > "κομιστέον περιεσκεπασμένον, ὅπως ἀπολευκανθῇ καὶ **τὴν δυσωδίαν ἀποβάλῃ**." (2.76.6)
    > *"It must be brought [into the sun] covered, so that it becomes white and **casts off its stink**."*
