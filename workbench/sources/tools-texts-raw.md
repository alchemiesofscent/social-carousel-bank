## Lenos

Based on the provided passages, the word **ληνός** (*lenos*) fundamentally refers to a **vat, trough, or press** used to hold or squeeze liquids. However, because of its distinct shape (a hollowed-out basin or receptacle), ancient medical and scientific writers frequently adapted the word metaphorically to describe anatomical structures and mechanical carvings. 

Here is a summary of what a *lenos* is, divided by its distinct usages in the text:

### 1. An Agricultural or Pharmacological Press / Vat (Literal Use)
In its most literal sense, a *lenos* is a large vat used for crushing grapes to make wine, or a pressing-vat used by pharmacologists to squeeze the essential oils and juices out of botanical ingredients (like flower petals).

*   **Example (Winemaking):** "...ἐπειδὰν ἐν ταῖς ληνοῖς αὐτῶν ἐκθλιβῇ πᾶς ὁ χυλός."
*   **Translation:** "...whenever all the juice is squeezed out of them [the grapes] in the vats (*lenoi*)."
*   **Citation:** Galen, *De alimentorum facultatibus* (Kühn vol. 6, p. 576)

*   **Example (Perfume/Oil Making):** "...κεχρίσθω δὲ καὶ ἡ ληνὸς μέλιτι."
*   **Translation:** "...and let the press/vat (*lenos*) also be smeared with honey."
*   **Citation:** Dioscorides, *De materia medica* (Book 1, chapter 43)

### 2. A Blood Reservoir in the Brain (Anatomical Metaphor)
The most frequent use of the word in these specific medical texts is anatomical. The Hellenistic physician Herophilus famously used the word *lenos* to describe the confluence of sinuses in the brain (known in modern anatomy via Latin translation as the *torcular Herophili* or "wine-press of Herophilus"). Galen and Oribasius describe it as a cavity or "cistern" at the top of the head where veins converge and pool blood before distributing it.

*   **Example:** "...συμβάλλουσι δὲ κατὰ τὴν κορυφὴν τῆς κεφαλῆς αἱ παράγουσαι τὸ αἷμα διπλώσεις τῆς μήνιγγος εἰς χώραν τινὰ κοινὴν οἷον δεξαμενήν, ἣν δὴ καὶ δι' αὐτὸ τοῦτο προσαγορεύειν ἔθος ἐστὶν Ἡροφίλῳ ληνόν..."
*   **Translation:** "...and the folds of the meninx that channel the blood converge at the top of the head into a certain common space, like a cistern, which for this very reason it is the custom of Herophilus to call a vat (*lenos*)..."
*   **Citation:** Galen, *De usu partium* (Kühn vol. 3, p. 708) *(Note: This exact anatomical description is also repeated in Galen's De anatomicis administrationibus and Oribasius's Collectiones medicae).*

### 3. The Floor of the Nasal Cavity (Anatomical Metaphor)
Because a *lenos* is a trough-like structure, poets and medical writers also applied the term to the hollow troughs or "floors" of the nasal cavities. 

*   **Example:** "...τὰ δὲ ἐδάφη τῶν κοιλωμάτων παρὰ τοῖς ποιηταῖς ὀνομάζονται ληνοὶ, τὰ δὲ ἑκατέρωθεν μέρη τοῦ χόνδρου τῆς ῥινὸς πτερύγια."
*   **Translation:** "...and the floors of the cavities [of the nose] are named troughs (*lenoi*) by the poets, and the parts on either side of the cartilage of the nose [are called] wings."
*   **Citation:** Theophilus Protospatharius, *De corporis humani fabrica* (Book 4, section 13)

### 4. A Carved Hollow in Medical Machinery (Mechanical Metaphor)
In the Hippocratic corpus, the word is used to describe a physical, trough-like depression carved into a wooden board. Here, the "Hippocratic bench" (a device for resetting fractured or dislocated bones) features smooth, grooved hollows designed to hold a wooden post or fulcrum in place.

*   **Example:** "...καταγλύφους δὲ, ὥσπερ ληνοὺς, λείας ἔχειν, τετραδακτύλους εὖρος καὶ βάθος..."
*   **Translation:** "...and to have smooth carved-out hollows, like troughs (*lenoi*), four fingers in width and depth..."
*   **Citation:** Hippocrates, *Vectiarius* (Section 38)


## louter

Based on the provided passages from Dioscorides’ *De materia medica*, a **λουτήρ** (*louter*) and its diminutive **λουτηρίδιον** (*louteridion*) refer to a **broad, shallow basin or tub**. 

While the word originally derives from the Greek root for washing or bathing (a wash-basin), in this pharmacological and perfume-making context, it functions strictly as a **maceration and steeping vessel**. Its specific wide and shallow shape makes it the ideal container for soaking massive quantities of delicate flower petals (like lilies, roses, and narcissus) in oil, allowing the preparer to easily reach in and mix the ingredients by hand without crushing them.

Here is a summary of its specific uses and senses in these texts:

### 1. A Broad, Shallow Steeping Basin (Primary Sense)
Dioscorides explicitly describes the physical dimensions of the *louter* when instructing how to steep delicate lily petals. It must be wide and shallow to allow for manual stirring and even distribution of the botanicals in the oil.

*   **Example:** "...κρίνα ἀριθμῷ χίλια ἀποφυλλίσας θὲς ἐν λουτῆρι πλατεῖ, μὴ βαθεῖ, ἐπιχέας τε τὸ ἔλαιον ἀνακίνει ταῖς χερσὶ μέλιτι κεχρισμέναις."
*   **Translation:** "...having stripped a thousand lilies of their leaves [petals], place them in a broad, not deep basin (*louter*), and pouring the oil over them, stir with hands smeared with honey."
*   **Citation:** Dioscorides, *De materia medica* (Book 1, chapter 52, section 1)

*   **Example (Narcissus infusion):** "...εἰς λουτῆρα ἀπόχει καὶ τοῦ ναρκίσσου ἔμβαλε ὡς ὅτι πλεῖστον τὸ ἄνθος..."
*   **Translation:** "...pour [the cooled oil] off into a basin (*louter*) and put in as many narcissus flowers as possible..."
*   **Citation:** Dioscorides, *De materia medica* (Book 1, chapter 53, section 1)

### 2. A Mixing Tub for Spices and Liquids
Beyond just soaking fresh flowers, the *louter* is also used as a mixing tub where dry aromatic spices (like cinnamon and saffron) and water are combined with previously pressed oils.

*   **Example:** "...κρόκου καὶ κιναμώμου τὸ ἴσον κόψας σήσας ἔμβαλε εἰς τὸν λουτῆρα μεθ' ὕδατος καὶ ἐπίχει τὸ πρῶτον ἐκπιεσθὲν μύρον..."
*   **Translation:** "...having pounded and sifted an equal amount of saffron and cinnamon, put them into the basin (*louter*) with water, and pour on the first-pressed ointment..."
*   **Citation:** Dioscorides, *De materia medica* (Book 1, chapter 52, section 4)

### 3. *Louteridion*: A Small Basin for Re-Maceration (Diminutive)
The text also uses the diminutive form, *louteridion* (a small basin or small tub). This is used when handling secondary pressings—taking the botanical matter that has already been squeezed in a basket/press and putting it back into a smaller tub to be soaked in oil a second time.

*   **Example:** "...βαλὼν δὲ εἰς λουτηρίδιον τὰ ἐξιπωθέντα ῥόδα ἐπίχει λίτρας ὀκτὼ οὐγγίας τρεῖς τοῦ ἐστυμμένου ἐλαίου καὶ πάλιν ἐξίπου."
*   **Translation:** "...and having put the pressed-out roses into a small basin (*louteridion*), pour on eight litrai and three ounces of the thickened oil and press it out again."
*   **Citation:** Dioscorides, *De materia medica* (Book 1, chapter 43, section 1)

## Bronze / Cauldron
Based on the provided passages, the word **χαλκός** (*chalkos*) and its related forms (such as *chalkeos*, *chalkous*, *chalkoma*) broadly refer to **copper or bronze**. However, in these ancient medical, pharmacological, and alchemical texts, the word takes on three highly distinct technical senses: **1. A vessel or instrument**, **2. A specific pharmacological ingredient**, and **3. A base elemental metal**.

Here is a summary of what *chalkos* represents in these texts, complete with examples, translations, and citations:

### 1. Hardware: A Copper/Bronze Vessel or Medical Instrument
In pharmacological recipes, *chalkos* frequently functions as a noun meaning a **copper boiling cauldron, vat, or storage vessel**. Copper was favored for boiling and storing certain medicines (especially eye salves) because the metal’s reactive properties were believed to improve the medicine. It is also used to describe medical instruments like cupping glasses and mortars.

*   **Example (A Boiling Vessel):** "...βαλὼν εἰς χαλκὸν ἕψε, μέχρις οὗ τὴν ὀσμὴν ἀναλάβῃ..."
*   **Translation:** "...putting it into a copper vessel (*chalkos*), boil it, until it takes on the fragrance..."
*   **Citation:** Dioscorides, *De materia medica* (Book 1, chapter 56, section 1)

*   **Example (A Storage Box/Pyxis):** "...ἕψε ἐν ἀγγείῳ χαλκῷ καὶ ἀπόθου εἰς πυξίδα χαλκῆν καὶ χρῶ."
*   **Translation:** "...boil in a copper vessel and store it away in a copper box (*pyxis chalkē*) and use it."
*   **Citation:** Galen, *De compositione medicamentorum secundum locos* (Kühn vol. 12, p. 738)

*   **Example (Medical Instruments):** "Παρὰ δὲ τὴν ὕλην διαφοραί εἰσι σικυῶν τρεῖς, ὑελαῖ, κεράτιναι, χαλκαῖ..."
*   **Translation:** "Regarding their material, there are three variations of cupping glasses: glass, horn, and bronze (*chalkai*)..."
*   **Citation:** Oribasius, *Collectiones medicae* (Book 7, chapter 16)

*   **Example (Mortar and Pestle):** "...εἰς θυίαν Κυπρίου χαλκοῦ, ἔχουσαν δὲ καὶ δοίδυκα ἀπὸ τῆς αὐτῆς πεποιημένον ὕλης..."
*   **Translation:** "...into a mortar of Cyprian copper (*chalkos*), having also a pestle made from the same material..."
*   **Citation:** Dioscorides, *De materia medica* (Book 5, chapter 79)

### 2. Pharmacological Ingredients (Processed Copper)
Copper was a staple astringent, caustic, and cleansing ingredient in ancient wound care, eye salves (collyria), and ulcer treatments. In the recipes, it almost never appears raw, but is chemically altered into specific forms:
*   **Χαλκὸς κεκαυμένος** (*chalkos kekaumenos*): Burnt/roasted copper.
*   **Χαλκοῦ λεπίς** (*chalkou lepis*): Copper scales or flakes.
*   **Χαλκοῦ ἄνθος** (*chalkou anthos*): Flower of copper (granulated copper oxide).
*   **Ἰὸς χαλκοῦ** (*ios chalkou*): Copper rust / verdigris.

*   **Example (Burnt Copper):** "ὁ δὲ κεκαυμένος χαλκός ἐστι καλὸς ὁ ἐρυθρὸς καὶ ἐν τῇ τρίψει κινναβαρίζων, ὁ δὲ μέλας πλεῖον ἢ δεῖ κέκαυται."
*   **Translation:** "As for burnt copper (*kekaumenos chalkos*), the red kind that turns cinnabar-colored when ground is good, but the black kind has been burnt more than is necessary."
*   **Citation:** Dioscorides, *De materia medica* (Book 5, chapter 76)

*   **Example (Copper Scales):** "ἥ τε γὰρ τοῦ χαλκοῦ λεπὶς ἰσχυρῶς ξηραίνει τε καὶ ῥύπτει καὶ μᾶλλον ἔτι ταύτης ὁ ἰός. ἄμφω δὲ ταῦτα δρᾷ καὶ διαβιβρώσκει τὰς σάρκας."
*   **Translation:** "For the scale of copper (*chalkou lepis*) powerfully dries and cleanses, and verdigris (*ios*) does this even more. Both of these are active and eat away at the flesh."
*   **Citation:** Galen, *De compositione medicamentorum per genera* (Kühn vol. 13, p. 714)

*   **Example (Flower of Copper):** "χαλκοῦ δὲ ἄνθος... ἄριστόν ἐστι τὸ εὔθρυπτον καὶ κατάπυρρον ἐν τῷ λεαίνεσθαι..."
*   **Translation:** "As for the flower of copper (*chalkou anthos*)... the best is that which is easily crumbled and fiery-red when ground up..."
*   **Citation:** Dioscorides, *De materia medica* (Book 5, chapter 77)

### 3. A Base Elemental Metal (Metallurgy & Alchemy)
In philosophical, anatomical, and alchemical passages, *chalkos* is discussed as a raw, hard elemental material, often compared alongside iron, lead, stone, and gold. In the alchemical texts of pseudo-Democritus (Bolus), it is a base metal used in dyeing or transmuting processes to simulate gold or silver.

*   **Example (Alchemical Transmutation):** "Καὶ ἐπίβαλλε λευκὴν γαίαν χαλκῷ, καὶ ἕξεις χαλκὸν ἀσκίαστον. Ξανθὴν δὲ ἐπίβαλλε σελήνην, καὶ ἕξεις χρυσὸν χρυσῷ..."
*   **Translation:** "And cast white earth upon copper (*chalkos*), and you will have unshadowed copper. And cast yellow upon silver, and you will have gold upon gold..."
*   **Citation:** Bolus (Pseudo-Democritus), *Physica et mystica* (Berthelot vol. 2, p. 44)

*   **Example (Physical Hardness):** "...φαίνεται μηδ' ὅλως ἑτέρῳ μολίβδῳ συμφυόμενος ἄνευ τοῦ θερμανθεὶς χυθῆναι." *(Context previous sentence: "ἵνα γοῦν παρῇ τις χαλκὸν καὶ σίδηρον καὶ χρυσὸν καὶ λίθον...")*
*   **Translation:** "[If one bypasses] copper (*chalkos*), iron, gold, and stone... [lead] clearly does not fuse with other lead at all without being heated and melted."
*   **Citation:** Galen, *De symptomatum causis* (Kühn vol. 7, p. 256)

## Sphyris

Based on the provided passages, the word **σφυρίς** (*sphyris* / *spyris*) refers fundamentally to a **basket**, typically made of woven wicker, reeds, or similar flexible materials. 

In ancient medical, pharmacological, and mechanical texts, it appears in two distinct technical contexts: **1. A strainer or pressing basket in oil manufacturing**, and **2. A receptacle used as a hanging weight in orthopedic traction**.

Here is a summary of its specific uses and senses in these texts:

### 1. A Strainer or Pressing Basket (Pharmacology)
In the recipes of Dioscorides, the *sphyris* is used during the extraction of oils from seeds or the pressing of infused botanicals. The crushed seeds or soaked flowers are placed inside the basket, and mechanical pressure (such as from a press or *organon*) is applied. The woven sides of the basket act as a sieve, allowing the liquid oil to seep out while holding back the solid pulp.

*   **Example (Extracting Castor Oil):** "...εἰς μύλην ἀποδόντες τοὺς κρότωνας ἀλήθουσιν ἐπιμελῶς, εἰς τὰς σφυρίδας δὲ ἐμβαλόντες τὸ ἀληλεσμένον δι' ὀργάνου ἐκθλίβουσιν."
*   **Translation:** "...handing the castor beans over to a mill, they grind them carefully, and placing the ground matter into baskets (*sphyridas*), they press it out by means of an instrument/press."
*   **Citation:** Dioscorides, *De materia medica* (Book 1, chapter 32, section 2)

*   **Example (Pressing Infused Cyprinum Oil):** "...ἐάσας τε ἀποβραχῆναι ἐξίπωσον διὰ σφυρίδος."
*   **Translation:** "...and having allowed it to steep, press it out through a basket (*sphyridos*)."
*   **Citation:** Dioscorides, *De materia medica* (Book 1, chapter 55, section 2)

*   **Example (Pressing Pine Oil):** "...ἀνελόμενός τε εἰς σφυρίδα ἐξίπου..."
*   **Translation:** "...taking it up into a basket (*sphyrida*), press it out..."
*   **Citation:** Dioscorides, *De materia medica* (Book 1, chapter 44, section 1)

### 2. A Receptacle for Counterweights (Orthopedics)
In the Hippocratic treatise *De articulis* (On Joints) and Apollonius of Kition's commentary on it, the *sphyris* is used as an improvised mechanical tool for setting dislocated hips. When the physician needs to apply continuous downward traction to the dislocated leg, they suspend a basket from the patient's foot/leg and fill it with heavy stones to act as a counterweight.

*   **Example:** "...ἐκ δὲ τοῦ σιναροῦ ἐς κεράμιον ὕδωρ ἐγχέαντα ἐκκρεμάσαι ἢ ἐς σφυρίδα λίθους ἐμβαλόντα."
*   **Translation:** "...and from the injured [leg], having poured water into an earthen pot, hang it, or having cast stones into a basket (*sphyrida*) [hang it]."
*   **Citation:** Hippocrates, *De articulis* (Section 78) *(Repeated almost verbatim by Apollonius of Kition in his commentary).*


## Kyrtis

Based on the provided passages, the word **κυρτίς** (*kyrtis*) and its diminutive **κυρτίδιον** (*kyrtidion*) refer to a **woven wicker basket used as a strainer or pressing-filter**. 

While the word in wider Greek literature often refers to a wicker fish-trap or birdcage, in these pharmacological texts it is used exclusively in a mechanical sense: it is the porous receptacle into which steeped flowers, crushed roots, or ground seeds are placed so that their oils and juices can be squeezed out while the solid plant matter is left behind.

Here is a summary of its use in the texts:

### A Woven Pressing-Basket / Strainer
The *kyrtis* is used in the extraction of both infused floral oils (like lily or basil) and heavy seed oils (like sesame). The plant material is scooped into the basket, and heavy pressure is applied—often by hand, or in heavier industrial processes, by a mechanical screw-press. 

*   **Example (Pressing infused lily oil):** "...πρωὶ δὲ ἀνελόμενος εἰς κυρτίδα ἐξίπου... μετεράσας δὲ ἐκ τοῦ κυρτιδίου τὸ ἐξιπωθὲν ἄρωμα εἰς τὸν λουτῆρα πάλιν ἐπίχει τοῦ ἠρωματισμένου ἐλαίου..."
*   **Translation:** "...and in the morning, taking it up into a basket (*kyrtis*), press it out... then, having transferred the pressed aromatic material out of the small basket (*kyrtidion*) into the basin, pour on the perfumed oil again..."
*   **Citation:** Dioscorides, *De materia medica* (Book 1, chapter 52, section 1-2)

*   **Example (Extracting sesame oil with a machine):** "Σησάμινον ἔλαιον γίνεται κοπτομένων ἢ ἀλουμένων τῶν σησάμων καὶ ἐκπιεζομένων διὰ κυρτίδων ἐπὶ κοχλίου..."
*   **Translation:** "Sesame oil is made by the sesame seeds being pounded or ground and pressed out through baskets (*kyrtides*) upon a screw-press..."
*   **Citation:** Paul of Aegina, *Epitomae medicae* (Book 7, chapter 20, section 11)

*   **Example (Extracting liquid for a panacea):** "...ἔπειτα εἰς κυρτίδα βαλόντες ἐκθλίβομεν τὸ ὑγρὸν εἰς ἕτερον ἀγγεῖον μυρεψικὸν, τὰ δὲ ἀφεψημένα ἀποῤῥίπτομεν."
*   **Translation:** "...then casting [the boiled botanicals] into a basket (*kyrtis*), we press out the liquid into another perfumer's vessel, and we throw away the boiled-down dregs."
*   **Citation:** Galen, *De compositione medicamentorum secundum locos* (Kühn vol. 13, p. 55)

*   **Example (Extracting juice from a root):** "χυλίζεται δὲ κοπεῖσα ἡ ῥίζα καὶ διὰ κυρτίδος ἐκθλιβεῖσα καὶ ξηρανθεῖσα ἐν ἡλίῳ..."
*   **Translation:** "The root is juiced by being pounded and pressed out through a basket (*kyrtis*) and dried in the sun..."
*   **Citation:** Dioscorides, *De materia medica* (Book 4, chapter 153, section 2)


## Psiathos

Based on the provided passages from Dioscorides and Oribasius (who quotes him closely), the word **ψίαθος** (*psiathos*) refers to a **woven mat made of rushes, reeds, or similar materials**. 

In these pharmacological texts, it appears in two distinct technical contexts regarding the collection and preparation of botanical materials: **1. A ground-cloth for harvesting resin**, and **2. A porous lid/barrier for infusing scented oils**.

Here is a summary of its specific uses in these texts:

### 1. A Ground-Mat for Harvesting Resin
In the harvesting of myrrh (*smyrna*), *psiathoi* are spread out on the ground beneath the myrrh tree. When the bark of the tree is cut, the liquid resin weeps out and falls onto these clean mats, preventing the precious sap from mixing with dirt and debris before it hardens.

*   **Example:** "...οὗ ἐγκοπτομένου ἀπορρεῖ τὸ δάκρυον εἰς ὑπεστρωμένας ψιάθους, τὸ δὲ τῷ στελέχει περιπήγνυται."
*   **Translation:** "...from which, when it is cut, the tear [resin] flows down onto rush mats (*psiathous*) spread underneath, while some congeals around the trunk."
*   **Citation:** Dioscorides, *De materia medica* (Book 1, chapter 64, section 1) *(Also repeated closely by Oribasius, Collectiones medicae, Book 12).*

### 2. A Porous Barrier / Lid for Vapor Infusion
During the preparation of quince oil (*melinon*), a loosely woven rush mat (*psiathos araia*) is placed across the mouth of a wide vessel containing hot oil. The quinces are laid on top of the mat and covered with blankets. The loose weave of the mat prevents the fruit from falling into the oil, while allowing the aromatic vapors from the fruit to pass down and infuse the oil below. It functions as an interchangeable alternative to a reed hurdle (*ripos ek kalamou*). 

*   **Example:** "...εἶτα ἀπηθήσας τὸ ἔλαιον βάλε εἰς ἀγγεῖον πλατύστομον, ἐπιθείς τε ῥῖπον ἐκ καλάμου ἢ ψίαθον ἀραιὰν ἄνωθέν τε ἐπιθεὶς κυδώνια μῆλα καὶ περικαλύψας ἱματίοις ἔασον ἡμέρας ἱκανάς..."
*   **Translation:** "...then having strained the oil, put it into a wide-mouthed vessel, and having placed upon it a hurdle of reeds or a loosely-woven rush mat (*psiathon araian*), and having placed quinces on top and covered them with cloths, leave them for a sufficient number of days..."
*   **Citation:** Dioscorides, *De materia medica* (Book 1, chapter 45, section 1)


## ὄργανον

ὄργανον fundamentally means “tool,” “implement,” or “apparatus.” In the passages relevant to perfumery, pharmacology, and alchemy, it does not mean a bodily organ. Instead, it denotes a technical device used to process aromatic or medicinal substances. Its main specialized sense here is a pressing apparatus for expressing oil, resin, or juice; more extended uses refer to a purpose-built fumigation device and to laboratory apparatus for drawing off liquid preparations. In this restricted set, the usage is literal and technical, not metaphorical.

Categorized senses

Pressing apparatus for extracting oils, resins, and plant juices

Here ὄργανον denotes the device by which pressure is applied to raw material in order to express a useful liquid. In direct perfumery contexts, this is the apparatus that yields fragrant substances such as stacte from myrrh; in related pharmacological contexts, it extracts medicinal oils or juices from seeds, berries, or roots.

Example: “τῆς προσφάτου σμύρνης τὸ λιπαρόν, κεκομμένης μεθ' ὕδατος ὀλίγου ἀποτεθλιμμένης τε δι' ὀργάνου. εὐώδης δὲ λίαν καὶ πολυτελής ἐστι … μύρον τὸ καλούμενον στακτή.”
Translation: “The oily part of fresh myrrh, after it has been chopped with a little water and pressed out by means of an apparatus. It is very fragrant and costly … the perfume called stacte.”
Citation: Dioscorides, De materia medica 1.60.1

Example: “εἰς τὰς σφυρίδας δὲ ἐμβαλόντες τὸ ἀληλεσμένον δι' ὀργάνου ἐκθλίβουσιν.”
Translation: “Putting the ground material into wicker baskets, they squeeze it out by means of an apparatus.”
Citation: Dioscorides, De materia medica 1.32.2

Example: “μύρτα μέλανα παρακμάζοντα λαβόντα κόπτειν καὶ ἐκθλίβειν τὸν χυλὸν δι' ὀργάνου …”
Translation: “One should take overripe black myrtle berries, crush them, and squeeze out the juice by means of an apparatus …”
Citation: Dioscorides, De materia medica 5.28.1

Example: “χυλίζεται δὲ κοπεῖσα ἡ ῥίζα καὶ διὰ κυρτίδος [καὶ ὀργάνου] ἐκθλιβεῖσα …”
Translation: “The root is made into juice when it is cut up and pressed through a basket [and apparatus] …”
Citation: Dioscorides, De materia medica 4.153.2
(Here ὀργάνου is bracketed in the edition, so the reading is textually uncertain.)

Fumigation apparatus for administering aromatics medicinally

Here ὄργανον means a specially constructed device, probably stool- or seat-like, designed to direct medicated vapor upward into the body. The aromatic substances used in the fumigation overlap with perfumery materials such as cassia, cinnamon, and myrrh.

Example: “ὄργανον χρὴ μηχανοποιέεσθαι, ἐφ' ὃ ἑζομένη εἴσεισιν ἀτμὸς ἐς τὰς μήτρας … ὑποθυμιῇν δὲ κασίην, κιννάμωμον, σμύρναν …”
Translation: “A device must be constructed on which, when the woman sits, vapor will pass into the womb … and one is to apply fumigation with cassia, cinnamon, and myrrh …”
Citation: Hippocrates, De mulierum affectibus 181

Alchemical apparatus for drawing off liquid medicines

In the alchemical passage, ὄργανα are laboratory devices used to draw off or separate liquid preparations. The term remains general, but the context suggests technical extraction equipment, possibly of a distillation or similar separation type.

Example: “ἐξ ὑγρῶν καὶ μέσων φαρμάκων διαιρέσεις, ἀνασπωμένων δι' ὀργάνων, καὶ μὴ ἀνασπωμένων, ἀλλ' ἢ διὰ ῥάκους ἐκθλιβομένων …”
Translation: “There are corresponding divisions among liquid and intermediate medicines, some being drawn off by means of apparatuses, others not, but instead being squeezed through cloth …”
Citation: Philosophus Anonymus Alchem., Περὶ τῆς θείας καὶ ἱερᾶς τέχνης φιλοσόφων, vol. 2, p. 439, l. 14

Summary of the isolated technical meaning

Across the perfumery-related and allied passages, ὄργανον consistently refers to the engineered means by which an aromatic or medicinal substance is made to yield something: oil, resin, juice, vapor, or extracted liquid. Its core meaning is therefore not “organ” but “processing apparatus,” with three main realizations in this corpus: a press, a fumigation device, and an extraction apparatus.





## μυροθήκη

Based on the provided passages, the word **μυροθήκη** (*myrothēkē*) refers to a concrete receptacle connected with perfume storage. In these texts it appears in two distinct technical contexts: first, as the perfume vessel itself, equivalent to an *alabastron* or similar unguent-container; second, as the outer case or casket that holds such perfume flasks.

### 1. The Perfume Vessel Itself

In several lexicographical notices, *myrothēkē* is the actual container that holds perfume or unguent. It is treated as equivalent to the *alabastron* and described as a smooth vessel, often of stone or glass, used specifically for storing perfume.

* **Example:** `<Ἀλάβαστρον>· ἄγγος μύρου μὴ ἔχον λαβὰς λίθινον. ἢ ψήφινος μυροθήκη.`

* **Citation:** Photius, *Lexicon* (alpha, entry 885)

### 2. A Case or Casket for Perfume Flasks

In another group of glosses, *myrothēkē* denotes not the flask itself but the case in which alabaster perfume vessels are kept. The word thus refers to a storage-case for *alabastoi*, that is, for the smooth perfume bottles used to contain the unguents.

* **Example:** `Ἀλαβαστοθῆκαι: αἱ θῆκαι τῶν ἀλαβάστων, ἃς ἐν τῇ συνηθείᾳ μυροθήκας καλοῦσι· ἀλάβαστοι δ' εἰσὶ λήκυθοι ὧν οὐκ ἔστι λαβέσθαι διὰ λειότητα.`
* **Translation:** “*Alabastothēkai*: the cases for alabaster flasks, which in ordinary usage they call perfume-cases; alabasters are flasks that cannot be grasped because of their smoothness.”
* **Citation:** Harpocration, *Lexicon in decem oratores Atticos* (p. 20, l. 6)





## μυροπώλης

Based on the provided passages, the word **μυροπώλης** (*myropōlēs*) refers to a man whose trade is the selling of perfumes. In these texts it appears both as the ordinary name for a perfume retailer or proprietor and as a more technical professional who stores, judges, and manages fragrant stock.

### 1. A perfume-seller or proprietor of a perfume business

In the clearest commercial passages, *myropōlēs* is simply the man who runs or owns a perfume business. The word can denote not only his occupation but also his established place in the urban market economy.

* **Example:** `οὗτ[ος] δὲ ὁ ἐκ τριγωνίας [ὢν] μυροπώλης, καθ[ήμε]νος δ' ἐν τῇ ἀγορᾷ ὅσαι ἡμέραι, τρία δὲ μυροπώλια κεκτημένος`

* **Citation:** Hyperides, *In Athenogenem* (col. 9)

### 2. A professional handler and keeper of perfume stock

In technical discussion, the *myropōlēs* is not just a shopkeeper but someone who knows how perfumes must be preserved. The trade therefore includes practical expertise in storage conditions and the maintenance of aromatic potency.

* **Example:** `διὸ καὶ οἱ μυροπῶλαι ζητοῦσι τὰς οἰκίας ὑπερῴους καὶ μὴ προσηλίους ἀλλ' ὅτι μάλιστα παλισκίους`
* **Translation:** “That is why perfume-sellers look for upper rooms that are not sunny, but as shaded as possible.”
* **Citation:** Theophrastus, *Fragmenta* 4.40

## μυρόπωλις

Based on the provided passages, the word **μυρόπωλις** (*myropōlis*) refers to a woman who sells perfumes. In these texts it appears both as the title of an individual female perfume-dealer and as the collective name for women associated with perfume service in festive settings.

### 1. A female seller of perfume

The noun can designate an individual woman whose occupation is the sale of perfume. In this usage it is the straightforward feminine counterpart to *myropōlēs*.

* **Example:** `νῦν δὲ πρὸς Αἴσχραν τὴν μυρόπωλιν ἰὼν πέντε λάβ' ἀργυρέας`

### 2. Perfume-selling women as a collective festive or service group

In comedy, the plural refers to perfume-women acting together in a scene of public festivity and organized abundance. The term still keeps its occupational force, but the setting is more performative and celebratory.

* **Example:** `κρατῆρας ἐγκιρνᾶσιν, αἱ μυροπώλιδες ἑστᾶσ' ἐφεξῆς`
* **Translation:** “The perfume-women stand in a row, mixing the kraters.”
* **Citation:** Aristophanes, *Ecclesiazusae* 841–842

## μυροπώλιον

Based on the provided passages, the word **μυροπώλιον** (*myropōlion*) refers to a perfume-shop or perfumery establishment. In these texts it appears in three distinct contexts: as a shop one visits in the city, as a commercial business that can be bought and sold, and as a metaphor for a repository of varied and choice material.

### 1. A perfume-shop as an urban place of resort

Here *myropōlion* means the actual shop to which people go, much like a barber’s shop or other workshop. It is part of ordinary city movement and sociability.

* **Example:** `ἕκαστος γὰρ ὑμῶν εἴθισται προσφοιτᾶν ὁ μὲν πρὸς μυροπώλιον, ὁ δὲ πρὸς κουρεῖον`
* **Translation:** “For each of you is accustomed to visit, one a perfume-shop, another a barber’s shop.”
* **Citation:** Lysias, *Pro invalido* 20

### 2. A perfume-shop as a commercial establishment or business asset

In legal and financial contexts, *myropōlion* can denote the perfumery establishment as something transferred, purchased, or burdened with debts. The word thus includes not just the room but the operating business.

* **Example:** `ἐλθόντες δ' ἐπὶ τὸ μυροπώλιον τὸ μὲν γραμματεῖον τιθέμεθα παρὰ Λυσικλεῖ Λευκονοεῖ· τὰς δὲ τετταράκοντα μνᾶς ἐγὼ καταβαλὼν τὴν ὠνὴν ἐποιησάμην`
* **Translation:** “When we came to the perfume-shop, we deposited the document with Lysikles of Leukonoe, and I paid the forty minas and completed the purchase.”
* **Citation:** Hyperides, *In Athenogenem* (col. 4)

### 3. By metaphor, a repository of varied and useful material

Later prose extends the concrete image of the perfume-shop into a metaphor for a place where many different fine or useful things are gathered together. The sense is still grounded in the idea of a shop stocked with assorted wares.

* **Example:** `καὶ περιφέρει γε τὴν ἱστορίαν ἐκ πολλῶν καὶ παντοδαπῶν τῶν ταῦτα εἰρηκότων ὥσπερ ῥῶπόν τινα ποικίλον καὶ χρήσιμον εἰς ἓν μυροπώλιον τὴν ἰδίαν ἐξήγησιν κατακεκλειμένην καὶ συνηγμένην`
* **Translation:** “And he brings together his history from many and varied sources, as though he had packed assorted and useful wares into a single perfume-shop within his own exposition.”
* **Citation:** Constantinus VII Porphyrogenitus, *De sententiis* (p. 72)

## μυροπωλεῖον

Based on the provided passages, the word **μυροπωλεῖον** (*myropōleion*) is another noun for a perfume-shop. In these texts it appears both as a literal place of sale and as a familiar worldly shop-type used in metaphorical contrast with sacred or serious settings.

### 1. A literal perfume-shop

In practical anecdotal use, *myropōleion* denotes the place where one goes to ask prices and buy perfume. It is a normal urban retail space.

* **Example:** `λαβὼν γὰρ αὐτὸν ἦγεν εἰς τὸ μυροπωλεῖον καὶ ἐπυνθάνετο πόσου τῆς κύπρου ἡ κοτύλη`
* **Translation:** “For he took him to the perfume-shop and asked how much a cotyle of cyprus-oil cost.”
* **Citation:** Teles, *Περὶ αὐταρκείας* (p. 13)

### 2. By metaphor, a secular and talk-filled shop-space

Christian authors use the word as shorthand for a noisy, ordinary, socially busy place, precisely in order to contrast such a setting with the dignity of the church. The metaphor depends on the recognizability of the perfume-shop as an everyday urban environment.

* **Example:** `Οὐ γὰρ κουρεῖον, οὐδὲ μυροπωλεῖον ἡ ἐκκλησία`
* **Translation:** “For the church is not a barber’s shop, nor a perfume-shop.”
* **Citation:** Joannes Chrysostomus, *In epistulam I ad Corinthios* (PG 61, p. 313, l. 35)

## μυροπωλεῖν

## διπλοῦν ἀγγεῖον μυρεψικόν

The term denotes a doubled working vessel in which a smaller container is set inside a larger one and the contents are heated indirectly. In the passages it belongs to several closely linked technical settings: perfumers’ work with aromatics and perfumes, physicians’ compounding of decoctions, oils, and salves, and the careful warming or melting of delicate ingredients.

### Full designation with *myrepsikon*

With *myrepsikon* the phrase marks the apparatus as belonging to the perfumer’s craft. The wording varies—διπλοῦν ἀγγεῖον μυρεψικόν, ἀγγεῖον διπλὸν μυρεψικόν, ἄγγος διπλοῦν μυρεψικόν—but the referent stays the same. In recipes it receives coarse-cut aromatics, roots, wine, oil, or water for soaking over several days and then for the next heated stage of compounding.

* **Example:** τὰ μὲν ἀρωματικὰ καὶ ξηρὰ κόψαντες εἰς παχέα καὶ βαλόντες εἰς ἄγγος διπλοῦν μυρεψικὸν καὶ τούτοις ἐπιβαλόντες οἶνον, ἐῶμεν βρέχεσθαι ἐπὶ ἡμέρας γʹ.
* **Translation:** Having cut the aromatics and dry ingredients into coarse pieces and put them into a double perfumer’s vessel, and having poured wine onto them, we leave them to soak for three days.
* **Citation:** Galenus, *De compositione medicamentorum per genera* vii, Kühn 13.1028.11.

### Generic ἀγγεῖον διπλοῦν and related vessel-words

When the texts stress construction rather than craft, the apparatus appears simply as ἀγγεῖον διπλοῦν or ἐπ’ ἀγγείου διπλοῦ. Galen defines it concretely as a smaller vessel inserted into a larger cauldron filled with boiling water. Related pharmaceutical texts may vary the container-word, using terms such as πύξις or σκεῦος, but the constant feature is the same doubled arrangement that keeps the material away from direct fire.

* **Example:** καλεῖται δ' οὕτως, ἐπειδὰν προϋποκειμένου λέβητος ὕδωρ ἔχοντος ζέον ἐνιστῆταί τι τούτῳ μικρότερον ἀγγεῖον, εἰς ὃ μέλλει τὸ ἔλαιον ἐγχεῖσθαι.
* **Translation:** It is so called when, a cauldron containing boiling water having been placed underneath, a smaller vessel is set into it, into which the oil to be heated is to be poured.
* **Citation:** Galenus, *De sanitate tuenda* vi, Kühn 6.290.1.

### *Diplōma* as shorthand for the same setup

The noun *diplōma* is the ordinary shorthand for the same apparatus, and therefore also for the operation carried out in it. Hence ἐν διπλώματι, ἐπὶ διπλώματος, and διὰ διπλώματος mean that something is to be boiled, melted, or warmed by the double-vessel method. This shorthand is widespread in recipes for oils, waxes, resins, fats, honey, and composite medicines.

* **Example:** διὰ διπλώματος ἕψων, ὅπερ ἐστὶν ἐπ' ἀγγείου διπλοῦ.
* **Translation:** by boiling by means of a *diplōma*, that is, on a double vessel.
* **Citation:** Galenus, *De compositione medicamentorum secundum locos* x, Kühn 13.37.4.

### Mild warming and melting in the double vessel

The double vessel is preferred when a substance must be softened or gently heated rather than exposed to harsh direct fire. The passages use it to warm ready-made perfumes before admixture and to melt wax, ladanum, turpentine, resins, and fats; elsewhere Galen states explicitly that perfumes lose their δύναμις if they are heated otherwise. In this use the apparatus serves controlled mild heat, not strong decoction.

* **Example:** τὸ μαλαβάθρινον μύρον, προχλιανθὲν ἐν διπλώματι ἐπὶ θερμοῦ ὕδατος.
* **Translation:** the malabathrum perfume, having first been made lukewarm in a double vessel over hot water.
* **Citation:** Aëtius, *Iatricorum liber* xii, ch. 55, ll. 43–44.


## Coatings, materials, and soaking

The passages treat three things separately: the vessel’s technical affiliation (*myrepsikon*), its construction as a double vessel (*diploûn*, *diplōma*), and its material or lining (*kassiterinon*, *geganōmenon tō kassiterō*). Those features can coincide, but they do not have to. In other words, a vessel may be called *myrepsikon* without being described as double, and a *diplōma* may be prescribed without any material being named.

### Tin and tin-treated vessels

The clearest material specifications are in the long perfume and medicated-oil recipes. One text requires “τὸ ἀγγεῖον μυρεψικὸν κασσιτέρινον,” a perfumer’s vessel characterized by tin, and immediately says to stopper it carefully and leave the contents standing for a fixed period (Aëtius, *Iatricorum liber* xii.55). A little later the same recipe calls for “ἕτερον ἀγγεῖον γεγανωμένον τῷ κασσιτέρῳ,” another vessel explicitly treated with tin, large enough to receive the must and allow it to rise in boiling (Aëtius, *Iatricorum liber* xii.55).

That is a real distinction in the wording. *Kassiterinon* marks the vessel by tin; *geganōmenon tō kassiterō* explicitly describes a vessel as coated or lined with tin. The text does not explain why tin is chosen; it simply prescribes it for definite stages.

The Galenic parallel shows that this is a stage-specific technical choice rather than the universal material of all such vessels. In the same broad type of preparation Galen first puts the soaked materials into “μικρὸν ἀγγεῖον μυρεψικὸν κασσιτερινόν,” but then prepares the must in a “μέγα χαλκεῖον” (a large bronze vessel), and only later gives the finished mixture two or three boilings in a double vessel (Galen, *De compositione medicamentorum per genera* vii, Kühn 13.1043–1044). Elsewhere Galen begins in a ceramic vessel and ends by transferring the finished compound into glass or silver (Galen, *De compositione medicamentorum secundum locos* x, Kühn 13.53; 13.55).

So the material specification belongs to particular operations, not to the term *myrepsikon* as such.

### Soaking for days is a regular technical stage

Yes: the texts repeatedly use these vessels for deliberate multi-day soaking. The verb is usually βρέχεσθαι, and the ingredients are characteristically cut coarse or thick before immersion: “εἰς παχέα κόπτεται,” “παχυμερῶς,” “ἁδρομερῶς.” This is not casual storage. It is a timed preparatory stage before heating, pressing, and recombination.

The durations vary by recipe. In one arterial compound the ingredients are “βρέχεται ἐν ἀγγείῳ διπλῷ μυρεψικῷ ἐπὶ ἡμέρας γʹ,” and on the next day the mass is boiled down to a third, then pressed out and filtered (Galen, *De compositione medicamentorum secundum locos* x, Kühn 13.55; cf. Aëtius, *Iatricorum liber* viii.56). In another recipe coarse aromatics are put into “ἄγγος διπλοῦν μυρεψικόν,” wine is poured over them, and they are left to soak for three days before oil is added and the mass is boiled (Galen, *De compositione medicamentorum per genera* vii, Kühn 13.1011). In the poplar preparation the maceration lasts nine days: the ingredients are kept “ἐν ἀγγείῳ διπλῷ μυρεψικῷ ... πάντα ἔα βρέχεσθαι ἐπὶ ἡμέρας θʹ,” then oil is added and the whole is boiled over a soft fire (Galen, *De compositione medicamentorum per genera* vii, Kühn 13.1022).

The longest maceration is in the large perfume recipe. Galen has the dry ingredients cut and sieved, then placed in a small tin perfumer’s vessel, carefully stoppered, and left “ἐπὶ δώδεκα ἡμέρας καὶ δώδεκα νύκτας” (Galen, *De compositione medicamentorum per genera* vii, Kühn 13.1043–1044). Aëtius transmits a shorter version of the same stage, only “ἡμέρας καὶ νύκτας δύο” (Aëtius, *Iatricorum liber* xii.55). The difference matters: the soaking period is recipe-specific and transmissible, not an automatic convention.

### How the vessel is used in practice

Across the recipes the sequence is strikingly regular.

First, the dry materials are cut coarsely rather than powdered. Then wine, water, or another liquid is poured over them in the vessel, and they are left standing for a prescribed number of days. After that the contents are heated, usually gently; the liquid is pressed or squeezed out; the spent solids are discarded; and the extracted liquid becomes the basis for the next stage.

In the longer perfume preparations the soaked mass is not merely left alone. It is stirred twice daily with a “σπάθη μυρεψική,” a perfumer’s spatula, and the spatula must be wiped carefully so that residue does not turn sour and damage the preparation (Aëtius, *Iatricorum liber* xii.55). The vessel is stoppered, covered, and carefully bound. This is a controlled maceration regime, not a passive hold.

After the maceration phase comes gentle heating. The doubled vessel is especially important here. Galen explains plainly what *diplōma* means: a smaller vessel set into a larger one containing boiling water (Galen, *De sanitate tuenda* vi, Kühn 6.290.1). He also says explicitly that perfumes are heated this way because their δύναμις is ruined if they are heated otherwise (Galen, *De methodo medendi* xiv, Kühn 10.573.14). In other words, the multi-day soak prepares the material; the double vessel then applies controlled heat without direct flame.

### What this shows about use

From the passages alone, the most secure conclusion is this: the vessel is not merely a container but a process tool. In these recipes it serves to

* hold coarse aromatics, roots, flowers, gums, wine, water, oil, or must;
* keep them under seal for a fixed maceration period;
* permit repeated stirring during that period;
* take the mixture into a stage of mild, indirect heating;
* allow pressing-out and transfer to another vessel for the next operation.

Tin or tin-treatment belongs to some of these stages, especially in long perfume and medicated-oil preparations, but the texts do not state the rationale. What they do show very clearly is that material, lining, and double construction are all meaningful technical variables, and that multi-day soaking in such vessels is a standard part of the work.

> κόψας τὰ ξηρὰ καὶ ἀραιοτέρῳ κοσκίνῳ σήσας βρέχε πάντα τῷ παλαιῷ οἴνῳ, τὸ δὲ βδέλλιον καὶ τὸ λάδανον προβρέχων καὶ λεάνας ἐν θυείᾳ ἐπίβαλε τοῖς βεβρεγμένοις· ἔστω δὲ τὸ ἀγγεῖον μυρεψικὸν κασσιτέρινον καὶ πωμάσας ἐπιμελῶς ἔα ἡμέρας καὶ νύκτας δύο. εἶτα τὴν σταφυλὴν προμεμαρασμένην λαβών, ἵνα μηδὲν σχῇ δρόσου, καὶ καθαρίως ἐκθλίψας καὶ διηθήσας ἐκ τῶν γιγάρτων ἔμβαλε τὸ γλεῦκος εἰς ἕτερον ἀγγεῖον γεγανωμένον τῷ κασσιτέρῳ· ἔστω δὲ μέγα, ὥστε καὶ εἰς τὸ ζέμα τοῦ γλεύκους χώραν ἔχειν ἱκανήν. εἶτα ἐπίβαλε τῷ γλεύκει πάντα τὰ ἐν τῷ παλαιῷ οἴνῳ βεβρεγμένα καὶ τὸ ἔλαιον καὶ σάλευε σπάθῃ μυρεψικῇ δὶς τῆς ἡμέρας· ἐπιμελῶς δὲ ἡ σπάθη ἀπομασσέσθω, ἵνα μὴ τὸ προσκείμενον αὐτῇ ὀξίσαν βλάψῃ τὸ φάρμακον. σκεπέσθω δὲ τὸ σκεῦος πώματι καὶ ἄνωθεν ἱματίοις καὶ κέντρωσιν ἀραιοῖς πλείοσι καὶ περιδείσθω ἐπιμελῶς, ὑποκείσθω δὲ τῷ ἀγγείῳ σπεῖρα ἑδράζουσα. ὅταν δὲ ἡμέραι ιεʹ διέλθωσιν, τὸ ἄμωμον ἀποδιδόσθω κεκομμένον καὶ πάλιν σαλευέσθω ὡς εἴρηται· τριακοστῇ δὲ ἡμέρᾳ ἐπὶ διπλώματος ἕψε καί, ὅταν γʹ ἢ δʹ ζέματα λάβῃ, καθελὼν ἀπὸ τοῦ πυρὸς ἐπίβαλε εὐθέως τὸ ὀποβάλσαμον καὶ ἀνακινήσας καὶ σκεπάσας καλῶς ἔα χλιαίνεσθαι. ... καὶ ποιήσας τὸ μύρον κατ' ἰδίαν ἐπίβαλε αὐτῷ τὸ μαλαβάθρινον μύρον, προχλιανθὲν ἐν διπλώματι ἐπὶ θερμοῦ ὕδατος.

**English**

> After cutting the dry ingredients and sifting them through a rather open sieve, soak them all in old wine; and after first moistening bdellium and ladanum and grinding them in a mortar, add them to the soaked materials. Let the vessel be a tin perfumer’s vessel, and after carefully stoppering it leave it for two days and nights. Then take grapes that have first been slightly withered so that they have no dew on them, press them cleanly, strain out the pips, and put the must into another vessel tinned with tin; it should be large, so that it has enough room for the must’s boiling up. Then add to the must all the ingredients that have been soaked in the old wine, along with the oil, and stir with a perfumer’s spatula twice a day; and the spatula must be carefully wiped, so that what clings to it, if it turns sour, does not damage the medicine. The container must be covered with a lid and with cloths above, and carefully bound round with several loose stitches; and a supporting coil should be placed beneath the vessel. When fifteen days have passed, the amomum is to be added, chopped more finely, and it is to be stirred again as directed. On the thirtieth day boil it in a double vessel; and when it has taken three or four boilings, remove it from the fire and immediately add the opobalsamum, then stir it and cover it well and let it grow warm. ... and after making the perfume separately, add to it the malabathrum perfume, first made lukewarm in a double vessel over hot water.

**Reference:** Aëtius, *Iatricorum liber* xii, ch. 55, ll. 37–44.

### 2. Galen on a small tin perfumer’s vessel, a large bronze vessel, repeated stirring, sealing, and later boilings in a double vessel

**Greek**

> βαλὼν εἰς μικρὸν ἀγγεῖον μυρεψικὸν κασσιτερινὸν, πωμάσας ἐπιμελῶς ἔα ἐπὶ δώδεκα ἡμέρας καὶ δώδεκα νύκτας. συγχρονισάτω δὲ τὸ γλεῦκος καθαρῶς σταφυλῆς ἐκτεθλιμμένης, ἵνα μηδὲν ἔχῃ δρόσου. βαλὼν δὲ τὸ γλεῦκος εἰς μέγα χαλκεῖον, ὥστε καὶ εἰς τὸ ζέμα τοῦ γλεύκους χώραν ἔχειν, ἐπιβάλλειν πάντα ἐν τῷ οἴνῳ βεβρεγμένα καὶ τὸ ἔλαιον καὶ τὸν οἶνον δὶς τῆς ἡμέρας ἐν σπάθῃ μυρεψικῇ σαλευέσθω ἐπὶ ὥραν. ἡ δὲ σπάθη ἀπομασσέσθω ἐπιμελῶς, ἵνα μὴ τὸ προσμεῖναν ἐν αὐτῇ ὀξῦσαν βλάπτῃ τὸ φάρμακον πάλιν καταθείσης τῆς σπάθης. ἐσκευάσθω δὲ τὸ σκεῦος πώματι καὶ ἄνωθεν κεντρῶσι πλείοσι καὶ περιδείσθω ἐπιμελῶς. ὑποκείσθω δὲ τῷ ἀγγείῳ σπείρα. ὅταν δὲ ἡμέραι ιεʹ. διέλθωσι, τὸ ἀμμωνιακὸν καὶ αὐτὸ τὸ ἄμωμον ἀποδιδόσθω, ὁλοσχερέστερον κεκομμένον. λʹ. δὲ ἡμέρας ἐν διπλῷ ἀγγείῳ ἐπ' ἀνθράκων δύο ἢ τρία ζέματα δεῖ ἐνδιδόναι, τῷ μύρῳ ἀποδιδόντας καὶ τὸ ὀποβάλσαμον.

**English**

> Put it into a small tin perfumer’s vessel, stopper it carefully, and leave it for twelve days and twelve nights. The must too should be prepared at the same time from grapes cleanly pressed, so that it has no dew in it. Put the must into a large bronze vessel, with enough room for the must to rise in boiling; add all the ingredients that have been soaked in the wine, together with the oil, and let the mixture be stirred with a perfumer’s spatula twice a day for an hour. The spatula must be carefully wiped, so that whatever remains on it, if it turns sour, does not damage the medicine when the spatula is put back again. The vessel must be fitted with a lid and above that with several coverings, and carefully bound around; and a coil-support should be placed beneath the vessel. When fifteen days have passed, the ammoniacum and the amomum are to be added, chopped more completely. On the thirtieth day, in a double vessel over coals, it must be given two or three boilings, while the opobalsamum is also added to the perfume.

**Reference:** Galenus, *De compositione medicamentorum per genera* vii, Kühn 13.1043.17–1044.6.

### 3. Galen on moving from a ceramic vessel to a double perfumer’s vessel, then to another perfumer’s vessel, and finally to glass or silver

**Greek**

> ... καὶ βάλλοντες εἰς κεραμεοῦν ἀγγεῖον, ἐπιβάλλομεν αὐταῖς ὕδατος γλυκέος ... καὶ θέντες ἐπὶ πυρὸς ἕψομεν. ὅταν δὲ εἰς ἥμισυ καταχθῇ, καθαιροῦμεν καὶ διϋλίζομεν εἰς διπλοῦν ἀγγεῖον μυρεψικόν. καὶ τὰς μὲν ῥίζας ἐκθλίψαντες ῥίπτομεν, εἰς δὲ τὸ ὕδωρ ἐπιβάλλομεν ἁδρομερῶς κεκομμένα ... εἶτ' ἐπὶ τὸ πῦρ ἐπιθέντες πάλιν ἕψομεν, καὶ ὅταν εἰς ἡμίσειαν καταντήσῃ, πάλιν διϋλίζομεν εἰς ἕτερον ἀγγεῖον μυρεψικὸν μικρόν. ... μεταίρομεν εἰς ἀγγεῖον ὑελοῦν ἢ ἀργυροῦν.

**English**

> ... putting them into a ceramic vessel, we pour sweet water over them ... and setting it on the fire we boil it. When it has been reduced to half, we skim it and strain it into a double perfumer’s vessel. After squeezing out the roots, we throw them away, and into the liquid we put the other ingredients, cut in coarse pieces ... then setting it on the fire again we boil it once more, and when it has come down to half, we again strain it into another small perfumer’s vessel. ... we transfer it into a glass or silver vessel.

**Reference:** Galenus, *De compositione medicamentorum secundum locos* x, Kühn 13.53.14 ff.

### 4. Galen on a three-day soak in a double perfumer’s vessel

**Greek**

> τὰ προειρημένα εἰς παχέα κόπτεται καὶ βρέχεται ἐν ἀγγείῳ διπλῷ μυρεψικῷ ἐπὶ ἡμέρας γʹ. τῇ δ' ἐπιούσῃ ἕψεται εἰς τὸ τρίτον, ἔπειτα εἰς κυρτίδα βαλόντες ἐκθλίβομεν τὸ ὑγρὸν εἰς ἕτερον ἀγγεῖον μυρεψικὸν, τὰ δὲ ἀφεψημένα ἀποῤῥίπτομεν.

**English**

> The ingredients mentioned above are cut into coarse pieces and soaked in a double perfumer’s vessel for three days. On the following day it is boiled down to a third; then, after putting it into a straining bag, we press the liquid out into another perfumer’s vessel, and the boiled-out solids we throw away.

**Reference:** Galenus, *De compositione medicamentorum secundum locos* x, Kühn 13.55.14 ff.

### 5. Aëtius, parallel three-day maceration followed by boiling in the double vessel

**Greek**

> τὰ προειρημένα ἅπαντα παχυμερῶς κόπτεται καὶ βρέχεται ἐπὶ ἡμέρας <γ>, τῇ δὲ ἑξῆς ἕψεται ἐν διπλώματι εἰς τὸ τρίτον, ἔπειτα ἐκθλίβομεν καὶ διηθοῦμεν.

**English**

> All the ingredients mentioned above are cut in thick pieces and soaked for three days; on the next day they are boiled in a double vessel down to a third, then we press them out and filter them.

**Reference:** Aëtius, *Iatricorum liber* viii, ch. 56, l. 126 ff.

### 6. Galen on coarse ingredients soaked for three days in a double perfumer’s vessel, then boiled with oils

**Greek**

> πάντα θλάσας καὶ βαλὼν εἰς ἄγγος διπλοῦν μυρεψικὸν, ἐπίβαλλε οἴνου τμωλίτου ξε. ηʹ. ἔα βρέχεσθαι ἐπὶ ἡμέρας γʹ. εἶτ' ἐπίβαλλε ἐλαίου Σαβίνου λίτρας ιστʹ. ἰρίνου λίτραν αʹ. σχινίνου λίτραν αʹ. Συριακοῦ λίτραν αʹ. γλευκίνου λίτραν αʹ. μυρσίνου λίτραν αʹ. δαφνίνου λίτραν αʹ. καὶ θεὶς ἐπὶ τὸ πῦρ ἕψε, ἔπειτα τὸ ὑγρὸν ἐκθλίψας τῶν ἡψημένων, ἐξέρα εἰς ἕτερον ἀγγεῖον καὶ πάλιν ἕψε.

**English**

> Crush everything and put it into a double perfumer’s vessel; pour in eight sextarii of Tmolian wine; leave it to soak for three days. Then add sixteen pounds of Sabine oil, one pound of iris oil, one pound of mastic oil, one pound of Syrian oil, one pound of must-oil, one pound of myrtle oil, and one pound of bay oil; and setting it on the fire boil it. Then, after pressing the liquid out from the boiled materials, transfer it to another vessel and boil it again.

**Reference:** Galenus, *De compositione medicamentorum per genera* vii, Kühn 13.1011.9 ff.

### 7. Galen on nine days’ soaking in a double perfumer’s vessel

**Greek**

> τοὺς ἀκρέμονας τῆς αἰγείρου κόψας ἐπιμελῶς, ἔχε ἐν ἀγγείῳ διπλῷ μυρεψικῷ καὶ τούτοις ἐπίβαλλε σάμψυχον, ῥίζαν σικύου κόψας παχυμερῶς καὶ τούτοις ἐπίβαλλε τὸν οἶνον καὶ πάντα ἔα βρέχεσθαι ἐπὶ ἡμέρας θʹ. τῇ δ' ἐπιούσῃ ἐπιβαλὼν τὸ ἔλαιον καὶ ἀνακόψας ἐπιμελῶς ἕψε, πυρὶ χρώμενος μαλακῷ.

**English**

> After carefully cutting the poplar catkins, keep them in a double perfumer’s vessel, and add sweet marjoram to them; after cutting the squirting-cucumber root in coarse pieces, add the wine to these, and leave everything to soak for nine days. On the next day, after adding the oil and stirring carefully, boil it, using a gentle fire.

**Reference:** Galenus, *De compositione medicamentorum per genera* vii, Kühn 13.1022.8 ff.

### 8. Galen’s definition of the double vessel

**Greek**

> καλεῖται δ' οὕτως, ἐπειδὰν προϋποκειμένου λέβητος ὕδωρ ἔχοντος ζέον ἐνιστῆταί τι τούτῳ μικρότερον ἀγγεῖον, εἰς ὃ μέλλει τὸ ἔλαιον ἐγχεῖσθαι.

**English**

> It is so called when, with a cauldron set underneath that contains boiling water, a smaller vessel is inserted into it, into which the oil that is to be heated is about to be poured.

**Reference:** Galenus, *De sanitate tuenda* vi, Kühn 6.290.1.

### 9. Galen on why perfumes are heated this way

**Greek**

> οἶσθα δὲ καὶ ὡς ἐπ' ἀγγείου διπλοῦ προϋποβεβλημένου κατὰ τὸ ἕτερον αὐτῶν τὸ μεῖζον ὕδατος ζέοντος, ἔθος ἡμῖν ἐστι θερμαίνειν τὰ μύρα· διαφθείρεται γὰρ ἡ δύναμις αὐτῶν ἑτέρως θερμανθέντων.

**English**

> You know too that we are accustomed to heat perfumes on a double vessel, with boiling water placed beforehand in the larger of the two parts; for their potency is destroyed if they are heated in another way.

**Reference:** Galenus, *De methodo medendi* xiv, Kühn 10.573.14.

## **ἴγδις / ἰγδίον**
1. **Overview**

**ἴγδις / ἰγδίον** fundamentally means a **mortar**: the receptacle in which substances are crushed, ground, rubbed, or worked. In the medical, pharmacological, and alchemical passages, that is the dominant and controlling sense. The feminine forms (**ἴγδις / ἴγδη**) and the neuter (**ἰγδίον**) are interchangeable here, and the word is often effectively synonymous with **θυεία** and **ὅλμος**. In these technical texts it can denote not only the mortar as a tool for grinding, but also the inner abrasive surface against which ingredients are levigated with liquid, and more broadly the working bowl in which compounds are mixed, strained, cooled, or kneaded. Outside that technical cluster, the dossier also preserves a broader container-sense and a figurative dance-name.

2. **Mortar proper: the grinding receptacle**

**Example:** «Ψιμύθιον οἴνῳ καὶ ῥοδίνῳ ἐν μολυβδίνῳ ἰγδίῳ καὶ μολυβδίνῳ δοίδυκι λειώσας…»

**Translation:** “Grind white lead with wine and rose-oil in a lead mortar and with a lead pestle…”

**Citation:** Paulus Aegineta, *Epitomae medicae* 3.59.5

**Example:** «εἶθ’ οὕτως λειοῖς αὐτὸν εἰς θυείαν ἤτοι ἰγδίον καθαρὸν…»

**Translation:** “Then grind it in a clean *thyeia*, that is, a clean mortar…”

**Citation:** Olympiodorus, *De arte sacra*, Berthelot–Ruelle II, p. 76, l. 14

3. **Mortar as a vessel/surface for wet levigation, washing, and purification**

**Example:** «κόψας αὐτὴν ἔμβαλε εἰς θυίαν καὶ ἐπιχέας ὕδωρ τρῖβε πλατείᾳ τῇ χειρὶ πρὸς τὴν ἴγδιν εὐτόνως…»

**Translation:** “Break it up, put it into a mortar, pour on water, and rub it vigorously with the flat of the hand against the mortar…”

**Citation:** Dioscorides, *De materia medica* 5.89.2

4. **Mortar as a working bowl for combining, receiving, cooling, or kneading compounds**

**Example:** «καὶ λαβὼν τὸ ὕλισμα ἕνωσον αὐτὸ μετὰ τοῦ φάβατος εἰς ἰγδίον… καὶ κίνησον αὐτά, ἕως οὗ ἑνωθῶσιν ἀμφότερα.»

**Translation:** “Take the expressed pulp and combine it with the bean mash in a mortar… and stir them until the two are fully united.”

**Citation:** *Hippiatrica*, *Excerpta Lugdunensia* 81.14–25

**Example:** «…καὶ μετὰ τὸ ἑψηθῆναι εἰς ἴγδιν ψυγέσθω. εἶτα τῇ χειρὶ μάλασσε καὶ χρῶ.»

**Translation:** “…and after it has been boiled, let it cool in a mortar. Then knead it with the hand and use it.”

**Citation:** *Hippiatrica*, *Hippiatrica Berolinensia* 130.48

5. **Extended literal sense: a vessel/container in general**

**Example:** «ἐὰν ἐν ἰγδίῳ ἤ τινι ἄλλῳ ἀγγείῳ ἐμβαλὼν ὕδωρ παρατιθῇς…»

**Translation:** “If, after putting water in a mortar or in some other vessel, you place it nearby…”

**Citation:** *Geoponica* 12.19.5

6. **Figurative/derived sense: the name of a dance**

**Example:** «μακτρισμὸς δὲ καὶ ἀπόκινος καὶ ἀπόσεισις καὶ ἴγδις ἀσελγῆ εἴδη ὀρχήσεων ἐν τῇ τῆς ὀσφύος περιφορᾷ.»

**Translation:** “Mactrismos, apokinos, aposeisis, and *igdis* are indecent kinds of dances involving the gyration of the hips.”

**Citation:** Julius Pollux, *Onomasticon* 4.101


## 
1. Fundamentally, **ἀλάβαστρον** denotes an **alabaster flask**, especially a smooth vessel used for perfume or ointment. In the supplied technical passages, that basic sense develops in three main directions: first, the **pharmacological container** for aromatic substances; second, the **stone/material itself**, valued for its cold, dense, scent-preserving properties and then used as a **medicinal ingredient**; and third, in alchemy, a **coded or symbolic white mineral/substance**, sometimes also an image for the purified white vessel or body. For semantic isolation here, the relevant field excludes the geographical and metrological occurrences and keeps the medical, pharmacological, and alchemical uses. I also include the close technical derivatives **ἀλαβαστρίτης** and **ἀλαβαστροειδής**, because they make the specialized sense explicit.

2. Distinct senses found in these technical contexts are as follows.

**Perfume or unguent flask**

**Example:** «διὸ καὶ εἰς ἀγγεῖα μολυβδᾶ ἐγχέουσι καὶ τοὺς ἀλαβάστρους ζητοῦσι τοιούτου λίθου· ψυχρὸν γὰρ καὶ πυκνὸν … Ὥστε δι' ἄμφω τηροῦσι, καὶ τῷ ψυχρῷ καὶ τῷ πυκνῷ, μήτε διιέντες ἔξω τὴν ὀσμὴν…»

**Translation:** “That is why they pour the perfumes into lead containers and seek alabastra made from such stone; for both lead and such stone are cold and dense … Thus they preserve them by both qualities, coldness and density, not letting the scent escape outward…”

**Citation:** Theophrastus, *Fragmenta* 4.41.

**Stone vessel specifically used for collecting aromatic oil**

**Example:** «ῥέουσι δὲ ἐξ αὐτοῦ ἐλαίου σταγόνες, ἃς ἐρίῳ ἀναψῶντες ἀπὸ τοῦ δένδρου ἀποπιέζουσιν εἰς ἀλαβάστρους λιθίνους.»

**Translation:** “Drops of oil flow from it; soaking them up with wool, they squeeze them from the tree into stone alabaster flasks.”

**Citation:** Ctesias, *Fragmenta* 45.47.

**Calcined alabaster as a medicinal ingredient**

**Example:** «ἄλλο. ♃ ἀλαβάστρου κεκαυμένου … κισσήρεως κεκαυμένου … ἁλὸς πεφρυγμένου … λεάνας ὡσαύτως χρῶ.»

**Translation:** “Another preparation: take burnt alabaster, burnt pumice, and roasted salt; grind them and use them in the same way.”

**Citation:** Galen, *De compositione medicamentorum secundum locos*, vol. 12, p. 866, line 13.

**Medicinal alabaster-stone as a therapeutic mineral**

**Example:** «λίθος ἀλαβαστρίτης ὁ καλούμενος ὄνυξ … κεκαυμένος ῥητίνῃ ἢ πίσσῃ διαφορεῖ σκληρίας, στομάχου δὲ ἀλγήματα μετὰ κηρωτῆς παρηγορεῖ· στέλλει δὲ καὶ οὖλα.»

**Translation:** “The stone called alabastrites, also called onyx, when calcined with resin or pitch disperses hard swellings; with wax salve it soothes pains of the stomach, and it also contracts the gums.”

**Citation:** Dioscorides, *De materia medica* 5.135.1.

**Alabaster-like sheen or whiteness as a medical descriptive standard**

**Example:** «ῥίζα ὁμοία σκορπίου οὐρᾷ, στίλβουσα ἀλαβαστροειδῶς.»

**Translation:** “Its root is like a scorpion’s tail, gleaming like alabaster.”

**Citation:** Dioscorides, *De materia medica* 4.76.1.

**Alchemical white stone used as a reagent**

**Example:** «Γινώσκεται γὰρ ὅτι ὁ λίθος ὁ ἀλαβαστρίτης ἐγκέφαλος κέκληται… Λαβὼν οὖν τὸν ἀλαβάστρινον λίθον, ὄπτα νυχθήμερον καὶ ἔχε ἄσβεστον.»

**Translation:** “It is known that the stone called alabastrites is called ‘brain’ … So take the alabastrine stone, fire it for a day and a night, and you will have quicklime.”

**Citation:** Zosimus, *Ζώσιμος λέγει περὶ τῆς ἀσβέστου*, line 2.

**Alchemical cryptonym for a lime-like or alkaline substance**

**Example:** «Ἀλάβαστρός ἐστιν ἄσβεστος, ἀπὸ τῶν φλοιῶν τῶν ὠῶν, καὶ ἅλας.»

**Translation:** “‘Alabastros’ means quicklime, from eggshells and salt.”

**Citation:** *Fragmenta Alchemica*, *Lexicon alchemicum*, vol. 2, p. 4, line 14.

**Metaphorical alabaster-like vessel/body in alchemical allegory**

**Example:** «κτίσαι σοι ναὸν μονόλιθον ψιμυθοειδῆ, ἀλαβαστροειδῆ…»

**Translation:** “Build for yourself a monolithic temple, white-lead-like, alabaster-like…”

**Citation:** Zosimus, *Περὶ συνθέσεως ὑδάτων πρᾶξις αʹ*, line 101.

In these passages, then, **ἀλάβαστρον** moves from a **literal perfume flask**, to the **stone substance from which such flasks are made**, to a **medical mineral**, and finally in alchemy to a **white reactive material and symbolic image of purified, sealed, white matter**.

## σπάθη

1. **Overview**

Fundamentally, **σπάθη** denotes a broad, flat, blade-like strip or plate. In these medical, pharmacological, and pharmaco-chemical passages, context determines the precise reference. In **ingredient lists**, especially after weights and measures, it refers to the **date-palm spathe**, that is, the sheath around the blossom or young fruit-cluster, treated as an aromatic/astringent raw material. In **procedural directions** with verbs such as **σάλευε** or **κινῶν**, it refers to a **flat spatula or paddle**, often made from palm material, used to stir or work a compound; in some recipes the palm implement is chosen specifically so that its own moisture or juice enters the preparation. In one **surgical-mechanical** passage, the word is extended again to a **flat slat or splint-like support** in an orthopedic device. No distinct metaphorical medical sense emerges from the excerpts. The later vernacular **“sword”** sense belongs to a different semantic branch, and the Procopius excerpt you supplied does not itself contain **σπάθη**.

2. **Date-palm spathe: the botanical sheath used as an aromatic/astringent ingredient**

   * **Example:**
     «<φοῖνιξ>, ὃν ἔνιοι <ἐλάτην> ἢ <σπάθην> καλοῦσι, περικάλυμμά ἐστι τοῦ καρποῦ τῶν φοινίκων ἀκμὴν ἀνθούντων· χρῶνται δὲ αὐτῷ οἱ μυρεψοὶ εἰς τὰς τῶν μύρων στύψεις.»

   * **Translation:**
     “‘Phoenix,’ which some call ‘elatē’ or ‘spathē,’ is the enclosing sheath of the date-palm fruit-cluster when the tree is in flower; perfumers use it to give perfumes an astringent quality.”

   * **Citation:**
     Dioscorides, *De materia medica* 1.109.4; cf. Oribasius, *Collectiones medicae* 12.phi.6; Aëtius, *Iatricorum liber xii* 55.11; Paulus Aegineta, *Epitomae medicae libri septem* 7.20.33–34.

3. **Stirring spatula or paddle used by perfumers and pharmacists**

   * **Example:**
     «βαλὼν εἰς κακκάβην ἕψει κινῶν σπάθῃ φοινικίνῃ ἀφῃρημένῃ τοῦ φλοιοῦ τοῦ χλωροῦ τοῦ ἔξωθεν αὐτῆς, ἵνα δυνηθῇ τῆς οἰκείας ὑγρότητος μεταδιδόναι τῷ φαρμάκῳ.»

   * **Translation:**
     “Put it into a pot and boil it, stirring with a palm-wood spatula stripped of its outer green bark, so that it may be able to impart some of its own moisture to the medicine.”

   * **Citation:**
     Oribasius, *Synopsis ad Eustathium filium* 3.5.2; cf. Aëtius, *Iatricorum liber xii* 55.31; Aëtius, *Iatricorum liber xv* 13.153–161; Galen, *De compositione medicamentorum per genera*, vol. 13, p. 1044, l. 6; Paulus Aegineta, *Epitomae medicae libri septem* 7.20.30; Photius, *Lexicon*, s.v. **Σπαθίδα**.

4. **Flat slat or splint-like support in a surgical apparatus**

   * **Example:**
     «ὁ μὲν βραχίων ἀσφαλίζεται πρὸς τὸ διάπηγμα [ἐν] τῇ τοῦ φοίνικος ἐπιδέσει, ὁ δὲ πῆχυς πρὸς τὴν σπάθην τῆς χειρὸς κεκαμμένης, ὅτε λοιπὸν στρεφομένου τοῦ ἐν τοῖς σκέλεσιν ἄξονος ὑπὸ τοῦ κάλου καθελκομένη ἡ χελώνη κατατείνει τὴν σπάθην.»

   * **Translation:**
     “The upper arm is secured to the frame with the palm-fibre bandage, and the forearm, with the hand bent, to the flat slat; then, when the axle in the legs is turned by the cord, the ‘tortoise’ being drawn downward puts the slat under tension.”

   * **Citation:**
     Oribasius, *Collectiones medicae* 49.26.8.

## λήκυθος / ληκύθιον 

**1. Overview of the Word's Meaning and Technical Adaptation**

The word **λήκυθος** (lekythos), alongside its diminutive **ληκύθιον** (lekythion), fundamentally refers to a flask, cruse, or bottle, usually characterized by a narrow neck and small mouth, which was used in antiquity primarily for storing and pouring oil, perfumes, and unguents. 

In the specialized texts provided, its meaning is adapted in three distinct ways:
* **In Medicine:** It serves as a vivid morphological analogy for anatomical structures and physiological anomalies, where the narrow neck of the vessel illustrates how fluids or fetuses can become trapped. It is also mentioned in psychiatric observations of patient delusions.
* **In Pharmacology/Therapeutics:** Physical components of the flask—specifically its papyrus stopper—are repurposed as therapeutic implements or molds in medical treatments.
* **In Alchemy:** The term undergoes a technical shift to describe laboratory apparatus. Here, it denotes small glass or earthen phials, retorts, or crucibles used by alchemists for heating, sublimating, and calcining chemical mixtures and metals.

***

**2. Categorized Senses in Medical, Pharmacological, and Alchemical Contexts**

### Metaphorical / Analogical Use in Anatomy and Physiology (Medical)
Hippocrates uses the physical properties of a leather or narrow-mouthed flask to explain physiological blockages, such as how congested blood remains trapped in veins or how a fetus in a transverse lie cannot exit the birth canal.

* **Example 1:** Ὥσπερ οὖν εἴ τις λήκυθον σκυτίνην στενόστομον ἐμπλήσας ἀλείφατος καταστρέψειεν ἐπὶ τὸ στόμα κατ' ἰθὺ, κἂν τοῦτο ποιήσειεν, οὐ δυνήσεται ἐξ αὐτῆς χωρέειν τὸ ἔλαιον... οὕτω δὴ καὶ ἐπὴν τὸ πλεῖον ὑγρὸν ἐν τῷ σώματι ὑπὸ τῆς ταραχῆς χωρίου λάβηται καὶ πλήσειε τὰς φλέβας, οὐκ ἔτι ἀπέρχεται ἐξ αὐτέων...
* **Translation:** "Therefore, just as if someone were to fill a narrow-mouthed leathern flask with oil and turn it straight upside down onto its mouth... the oil will not be able to flow out of it... so too, whenever excess fluid in the body is trapped in a region due to a disturbance and fills the veins, it no longer departs from them..."
* **Citation:** Hippocrates et Corpus Hippocraticum Med., *De semine, de natura pueri, de morbis iv*, Section 51, line 63

* **Example 2:** Ὧδε δὲ γίνεται τὸ πάθημα· ὥσπερ εἴ τις ἐς λήκυθον σμικρόστομον πυρῆνα ἐμβάλοι, οὐκ εὐφυὲς ἐξελεῖν πλαγιεύμενον, οὕτω δὴ καὶ τῇ γυναικὶ χαλεπὸν πάθημα τὸ ἔμβρυον, ἐπειδὰν λοξωθῇ, οὐκ ἔξεισι γάρ.
* **Translation:** "This is how the affliction happens: just as if someone were to drop a fruit-stone into a small-mouthed flask, it is not naturally easy to remove if it turns sideways; so too is the fetus a difficult affliction for the woman when it turns transversely, for it will not come out."
* **Citation:** Hippocrates et Corpus Hippocraticum Med., *De mulierum affectibus i–iii*, Section 33, line 5

### Clinical Observation of Mental Pathology (Psychiatric / Medical)
The physician Aretaeus references the flask as the subject of a specific hallucination or phobia experienced by a melancholic or manic patient.

* **Example:** γίγνονται δὲ καὶ ἀλλόκοτοι φαντασίαι. ἐδεδίει γάρ τις ληκύθων ἔκπτωσιν, καὶ ἄλλος οὐκ ἔπινε, δοκέων ἑωυτὸν πλίνθον ἔμμεναι...
* **Translation:** "Bizarre hallucinations also occur. For one person feared the falling [and breaking] of oil-flasks, and another would not drink, believing himself to be a brick..."
* **Citation:** Aretaeus Med., *De causis et signis acutorum morborum*, Book 1, chapter 6, section 5, line 8

### Pharmacological and Therapeutic Implements 
In Late Antique medical treatises, parts of the *lekythos* are integrated directly into pharmaceutical recipes and physical therapies. In this instance, a papyrus stopper is utilized to create a structural mold for treating a newborn's navel.

* **Example:** Λαβὼν βύσμα ἀπὸ ληκύθου βίβλινον περιείλησον ὀθονίῳ, ἔπειτα μάνναν ὠοῦ τῷ λευκῷ ἀναλαβὼν κατάχρισον καὶ ἔνθες εἰς τὸν ὀμφαλὸν καὶ ἐπίδει προστύπως.
* **Translation:** "Taking a papyrus stopper from an oil-flask, wrap it in a linen cloth, then taking incense dust mixed with egg white, smear it on, place it into the navel, and bind it so that it forms a mold."
* **Citation:** Pseudo-Galenus Med., *De remediis parabilibus libri iii*, Volume 14, page 483, line 10 *(Identical instruction also found in Oribasius Med., Libri ad Eunapium 4.115.6)*

### Laboratory Apparatus for Heating and Sublimation (Alchemical)
In alchemical recipes, the term refers to the actual laboratory glassware or pottery (*lekythion*) used to contain volatile compounds, minerals, and metals during exposure to prolonged heat, such as in sand-baths or over a *kerotakis* (a sublimatory palette).

* **Example 1:** Τινὲς τῷ παντὶ ὀστράκῳ ἐν ὑαλοῖς κύβοις ἑψοῦσιν καὶ ὀπτῶσιν ἐπὶ τῆς κηροτακίδος· καὶ ταῦτα καλοῦσιν ληκύθια.
* **Translation:** "Some boil and roast the whole earthen substance in glass cubes upon the kerotakis; and these they call little flasks."
* **Citation:** Zosimus Alchem., *Περὶ σταθμοῦ ξανθώσεως*, Volume 2, page 183, line 14

* **Example 2:** Καὶ λαβὼν κιννάβαριν, κινναβάρισον ἐλαίῳ ῥεφανίνῳ εἰς ληκύθιον, καὶ ἀσφαλισάμενος, θὲς ἐν χώστρᾳ ὥρας ιʹ...
* **Translation:** "And taking cinnabar, mix the cinnabar with radish oil into a little flask, and having sealed it securely, place it in a sand-bath for 10 hours..."
* **Citation:** Moses Alchem., *Εὐποία καὶ εὐτυχία τοῦ κτισαμένου...*, Volume 2, page 300, line 19

* **Example 3:** ...καὶ βάλε εἰς ὑέλεον ληκύθιον, ὑποστρώσας σινωπίδος, καὶ χαλκάνθου ἐξ ἴσου· ὁμοῦ λειώσας...
* **Translation:** "...and cast it into a small glass flask, having laid down a base of equal parts red ochre and copper-vitriol, triturated together..."
* **Citation:** Iamblichus Alchem., *Fragmenta alchemica*, Volume 2, page 287, line 4
