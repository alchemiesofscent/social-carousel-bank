# Galen on Perfumes in drugs

In these passages, Galen treats μύρα not as “perfume” in the narrow modern sense, but as perfumed or aromatic medicated oils and ointments. Their medical role is consistent: they can be active drugs in their own right, carriers for other ingredients, or delivery systems through smell, topical application, ear-instillation, and even oral dosing. Just as important, Galen distinguishes medical perfumery from merely cosmetic fragrance. Translations below are mine.

1. Galen first defines the pharmaceutical distinction between plain oil and perfume.

> ἔλαιον μὲν γὰρ ῥόδινον ὀνομάζουσιν τὸ χωρὶς τῶν ἀρωμάτων σκευασθέν. ὅτῳ δ’ ἐπεμβέβληταί τι καὶ τούτων, οὐκ ἔλαιον ἔτι τὸ τοιοῦτον, ἀλλὰ μύρον ἤδη προσαγορεύουσιν.

Translation: “They call ‘rose oil’ that which has been prepared without aromatics; but when some of these have been added, the preparation is no longer an oil, but is now called a perfume.”
Citation: *De simplicium medicamentorum temperamentis ac facultatibus* XI, Kühn 11.538.4.

This is the basic point: perfume is a pharmacological form, not just a smell.

2. He explicitly says that oils become perfumes by being combined with aromatics, and that their properties are altered by the added ingredients.

> λέγω δὲ ῥοδίνου τε καὶ μηλίνου καὶ κρινίνου… τούτων γὰρ ἕκαστον, ὅταν ἅμα τοῖς ἀρώμασι σκευασθῇ, μύρον ἀποτελεῖται… κατὰ φύσιν ἑκάστου τῶν ἐμβληθέντων ἀλλοιωθήσεται τὸ ἔλαιον.

Translation: “I mean rose, apple, lily, and similar oils… for each of these, when prepared together with aromatics, becomes a perfume… and the oil will be altered according to the nature of each thing added.”
Citation: *De simpl. med.* XI, Kühn 11.872.11.

So for Galen, perfume is therapeutically meaningful because the base oil takes on the nature of the admixture.

3. Perfumes can themselves function as simple drugs. In the entry on lily, both the oil and the perfume are assigned definite therapeutic powers.

> τοὔλαιον τὸ ἐξ αὐτοῦ καὶ τὸ μύρον ἀδήκτου διαφορητικῆς τε καὶ μαλακτικῆς ἐστι δυνάμεως, ἐξ ὧνπερ καὶ ταῖς ἐν μήτραις σκληρότησιν ἐπιτηδειότατόν ἐστιν.

Translation: “The oil made from it and the perfume have a non-irritating dispersive and softening power; for that reason they are especially suited to indurations of the womb.”
Citation: *De simpl. med.* XI, Kühn 12.45.14.

The same passage also gives rose oil as a vehicle in wound care:

> τὴν μὲν οὖν ῥίζαν ὀπτήσαντες, εἶθ’ ἅμα ῥοδίνῳ λειώσαντες ἐπιτιθέασι τοῖς κατακαύμασιν ἄχρι συνουλώσεως.

Translation: “They roast the root, then grind it with rose oil and apply it to burns until healing.”
Citation: *De simpl. med.* XI, Kühn 12.45.14.

4. Perfumes can work through smell. In head complaints caused by cold, Galen preserves and explains treatment by anointing the nostrils with warming perfumes.

> ἀξιοῖ διαχρίειν τοῖς τοιούτοις μύροις τὰς ῥῖνας, ὡς διὰ τῆς ὀσμῆς αὐτῶν θερμαινομένης τῆς κεφαλῆς.

Translation: “He recommends anointing the nostrils with such perfumes, so that the head is warmed by their smell.”
Citation: *De compositione medicamentorum secundum locos* X, Kühn 12.542.2–543.1.

This is a clear example of olfactory administration as a medical route, not an incidental pleasant odor.

5. In ear medicine, Galen uses perfumes as carefully selected therapeutic liquids, and he insists on pharmaceutical quality control.

> ἡ πυρία δέ σοι γινέσθω… διὰ τῶν ἀνωδύνων, ἅπερ ἐστὶ τό τε κάλλιστον νάρδινον μύρον… καὶ μετὰ τοῦτο τὸ Κομμαγηνόν τε καὶ Σούσινον… λύειν οὖν αὐτὰ διά τε τοῦ ναρδίνου μύρου καὶ, εἰ θέρος εἴη… διὰ ῥοδίνου καλοῦ.

Translation: “Let the fomentation be made with the least painful agents, namely the finest nard perfume… and next the Commagene and susinum perfumes… these should be thinned either with nard perfume or, if it is summer, with good rose oil.”
Citation: *De comp. med. sec. loc.* X, Kühn 12.604.10.

He immediately defines what “good” rose oil means:

> μέμνησο δ’ ὅτι καλὸν ἀεὶ λέγω ῥόδινον… χωρὶς ἁλῶν ἐσκευασμένον, οὐδενὸς ἄλλου προςεμβεβλημένου… ἕνεκα τοῦ πολλῷ χρόνῳ διαμεῖναι τὸ ῥόδινον εὐῶδες.

Translation: “Remember that by ‘good’ rose oil I always mean one prepared without salts and with nothing else added… as some people do in order to make the rose oil stay fragrant for a long time.”
Citation: *De comp. med. sec. loc.* X, Kühn 12.604.14.

This is a key Galenic move: he rejects perfume-additives meant only to preserve scent when they interfere with medicinal purity.

6. Perfumes can also be given internally. That is one of the strongest signs that μύρα in Galen are drugs, not cosmetics.

For liver disease:

> ἢ νάρδου μύρου κοχλιάρια δʹ. πίνειν δίδου καθ’ ἡμέραν, καὶ μάλιστα ἐπὶ τῶν σκιῤῥουμένων.

Translation: “Or give four spoonfuls of nard perfume to drink daily, especially in cases of induration.”
Citation: *De comp. med. sec. loc.* X, Kühn 13.217.18.

For cantharides poisoning:

> ἢ πήγανον λεάνας καὶ ἰρίνῳ μύρῳ μίξας δίδου πίνειν.

Translation: “Or grind rue, mix it with iris perfume, and give it to drink.”
Citation: *De antidotis* II, Kühn 14.141.15.

7. Galen also marks a boundary: not every perfume recipe belongs to medicine.

> καταπλάσματα τῶν ἀρωμάτων ἐν ἱματίοις… θυμιαμάτων τε παντοίων ποικιλίας καὶ χρισμάτων καὶ μύρων, ἔξω γὰρ ταῦτα τῆς ἰατρικῆς.

Translation: “Perfume-poultices for garments… varieties of fumigations and of ointments and perfumes—these lie outside medicine.”
Citation: *De comp. med. sec. loc.* X, Kühn 12.450.2.

That line helps frame the whole dossier. Galen is not interested in perfume culture as such. He keeps perfumes only where they preserve health, relieve pain, soften, disperse, heat, cool, or carry other drugs effectively.

So the short conclusion is this: for Galen, perfumes are legitimate pharmaceutical substances. They are medicated aromatic oils whose efficacy depends on composition, temperament, route of use, and purity. He uses them as simple remedies, as vehicles in compound drugs, by smell, by local application, and by mouth, while sharply excluding merely luxurious or cosmetic fragrance from medicine.

Agreed. That passage should be cited in full. Below is a tighter source sheet, limited to the passages that best show Galen treating perfumes as pharmaceutical substances rather than as mere luxuries. Translations are mine.

1. The core distinction: plain oil versus perfume.

Greek

> Νυνὶ δὲ περὶ ῥοδίνου βραχέα προσθεὶς ἐπὶ τοῖς κατ' ἀρχὰς εἰρημένοις ἐν τῷδε καταπαύσω τὸν λόγον. εἴρηται μὲν οὖν ὡς οὔτ' ὀσμαῖς οὔτε χρόαις οὔτ' ἄλλῳ τινὶ τῶν συμβεβηκότων εὔλογόν ἐστι προσέχειν τὸν νοῦν ἐν ταῖς τῶν δυνάμεων ἐξετάσεσιν, ἀλλὰ τοῖς ἐναργῶς φαινομένοις ἐπί τε τῶν ὑγιαινόντων καὶ νοσούντων σωμάτων. ἀποδέδεικται δὲ καὶ ὡς τοὔλαιον, ὅταν ἄναλον ᾖ καὶ καθαρὸν ἀκριβῶς, ἐκ τῆς μέσης ὕλης ἐστίν. ὥστε καὶ τὸ ῥόδινον χρὴ τὸ ἀκριβὲς καὶ ἁπλοῦν καὶ ὄντως ῥόδινον ἐξ ἐλαίου τε τούτου κατεσκευάσθαι καὶ μηδὲν ἐν ἑαυτῷ φαρμακωδέστερον ἔχειν, ὥσπερ εἰώθασιν οἱ μυρεψοὶ σκευάζειν, ὑπὲρ τοῦ μόνιμόν τε καὶ εὐωδέστερον γενέσθαι προστύφοντες ἀρώμασι τοὔλαιον πρὶν ἐμβαλεῖν τὰ ῥόδα. κάλλιον δὲ ἴσως ἐστὶ καὶ τοῖς ὀνόμασιν διαστέλλειν αὐτὰ, καθότι καὶ τοὺς παλαιοὺς οἶδα ποιοῦντας. ἔλαιον μὲν γὰρ ῥόδινον ὀνομάζουσιν τὸ χωρὶς τῶν ἀρωμάτων σκευασθέν. ὅτῳ δ' ἐπεμβέβληταί τι καὶ τούτων, οὐκ ἔλαιον ἔτι τὸ τοιοῦτον, ἀλλὰ μύρον ἤδη προσαγορεύουσιν. ὥστε ἐπειδὴ τὸ μὲν ἔλαιον οἷον ὕλη τίς ἐστιν τῶν θερμαινόντων τε καὶ ψυχόντων φαρμάκων ἐκ τῆς μέσης κράσεως ὑπάρχον, ἐξ ἐλαίου δὲ καὶ ῥόδων χυλῶν σύγκειται τὸ ῥόδινον, ἔστι δήπου καὶ αὐτὸ τοιοῦτον τὴν κρᾶσιν οἷός περ καὶ ὁ τῶν ῥόδων χυλός. ἔστι δὲ δήπου ῥᾷστον οὐ ῥόδων χυλοῦ μόνον αὐτοῦ καθ' ἑαυτὸν, ἀλλὰ καὶ συνθέντας ἔλαιον ῥόδινον ἐπί τε τῶν ὑγιαινόντων καὶ νοσούντων, ὡς ἤδη πολλάκις εἴρηται, τὴν βάσανον αὐτοῦ ποιήσασθαι τῆς δυνάμεως. εὑρήσεις οὖν ἐμψυχομένους μὲν ὑπ' αὐτοῦ τοὺς ἔτι κεκαυμένους καὶ παραχρῆμά τινος ὠφελείας αἰσθανομένους, βλαπτομένους δ' ἐναργῶς ἅπαντας τοὺς ἐψυγμένους, ὥστε σε σαφῶς ἑκατέρωθεν συνιέναι τῆς δυνάμεως αὐτοῦ ψυχούσης μᾶλλον ἢ θερμαινούσης. οὐ μὴν ἰσχυρά γε ἡ ἀπὸ ῥοδίνου φαίνεταί σοι ψύξις, ἀλλ' ἐγγυτάτω πως εἶναι δόξει τῆς μέσης κράσεως. ὀνομάζω δὲ τὰ τοιαῦτα φάρμακα σαφοῦς ἕνεκα διδασκαλίας ἐκ τῆς πρώτης φύσεώς τε καὶ τάξεως τῶν ψυχόντων, ὥσπερ αὖ καὶ ὅσα βραχὺ τῆς μέσης κράσεως ἐπὶ τὸ θερμότερον ἀποκεχώρηκεν, ἐκ τῆς πρώτης ἀποστάσεώς τε καὶ τάξεως τῶν θερμαινόντων.

Translation
“Now I will add a few remarks about rose oil to what was said at the beginning and bring this discussion to a close. It has been said that in investigating powers one ought to attend neither to smells nor to colors nor to any other incidental property, but to what appears plainly in healthy and diseased bodies. It has also been shown that oil, when unsalted and perfectly pure, belongs to the neutral substrate. Accordingly, the exact, simple, and genuinely rose preparation should be made from this oil and contain nothing more medicinally active in itself, unlike the way perfumers usually prepare it, first astringing the oil with aromatics before adding the roses, so that it may last longer and smell better. Perhaps it is better also to distinguish them by name, as I know the ancients did. For they call ‘rose oil’ that which has been prepared without aromatics; but when some of these too have been added, such a thing is no longer oil, but is now called a perfume. Therefore, since oil is like a material substrate for heating and cooling drugs, possessing a neutral mixture, and since the rose preparation is composed of oil and the juices of roses, it too must have a mixture like that of rose juice. And it is of course easiest to test the power not only of rose juice by itself, but also of compounded rose oil, on both healthy and diseased persons, as has often been said. You will find that those still overheated are refreshed by it and immediately perceive some benefit, whereas all who are chilled are plainly harmed by it; thus you will clearly understand from both sides that its power is more cooling than heating. Yet the cooling imparted by rose oil does not appear strong; rather it seems to stand very close to the neutral mixture. For clarity in teaching, I classify such drugs in the first rank of coolants, just as those that deviate a little from the neutral mixture toward warmth belong to the first rank of heating agents.”

This is the central text. It gives the formal distinction between ἔλαιον and μύρον, criticizes perfumers’ stabilizing additives, and insists that medicinal evaluation depends on bodily effect, not scent.
Reference: Galen, *De simplicium medicamentorum temperamentis ac facultatibus* XI, Kühn 11.538.4.

2. General rule: oils become perfumes when prepared with aromatics.

Greek

> λέγω δὲ ῥοδίνου τε καὶ μηλίνου καὶ κρινίνου καὶ ὅσα τοιαῦτα καρπῶν ἢ ἀνθῶν ἢ βλαστῶν ἢ φύλλων ἐναποβρεχομένων ἐν ἐλαίῳ γίγνεται. τούτων γὰρ ἕκαστον, ὅταν ἅμα τοῖς ἀρώμασι σκευασθῇ, μύρον ἀποτελεῖται… κατὰ φύσιν ἑκάστου τῶν ἐμβληθέντων ἀλλοιωθήσεται τὸ ἔλαιον.

Translation
“I mean rose, apple, lily, and similar oils produced by soaking fruits, flowers, shoots, or leaves in oil. For each of these, when prepared together with aromatics, becomes a perfume… and the oil will be altered according to the nature of each of the ingredients added.”

This generalizes the distinction beyond rose oil: perfume is a medicated oil whose properties depend on its additives.
Reference: Galen, *De simpl. med.* XI, Kühn 11.872.11.

3. Fragrance does not equal therapeutic strength.

Greek

> ἡ μὲν κληματῖτις εὐωδεστέρα μὲν, ὥστε καὶ πρὸς τὰ μύρα χρῆσθαι τοὺς μυρεψοὺς αὐτῇ, πρὸς τὰς ἰάσεις δ' ἀσθενεστέρα.

Translation
“The clematitis-kind is more fragrant, so that perfumers even use it for perfumes, but for treatments it is weaker.”

This is useful because it shows Galen explicitly separating perfumery-value from medical efficacy.
Reference: Galen, *De simpl. med.* XI, Kühn 11.836.1.

4. Perfumes as active medicaments.

Greek

> ὅθεν καὶ τοὔλαιον τὸ ἐξ αὐτοῦ καὶ τὸ μύρον ἀδήκτου διαφορητικῆς τε καὶ μαλακτικῆς ἐστι δυνάμεως, ἐξ ὧνπερ καὶ ταῖς ἐν μήτραις σκληρότησιν ἐπιτηδειότατόν ἐστιν…
> τὴν μὲν οὖν ῥίζαν ὀπτήσαντες, εἶθ' ἅμα ῥοδίνῳ λειώσαντες ἐπιτιθέασι τοῖς κατακαύμασιν ἄχρι συνουλώσεως.

Translation
“Accordingly, the oil made from it and the perfume have a non-irritating dispersive and softening power; for that reason they are especially suited to indurations of the womb… They roast the root, then grind it with rose oil and apply it to burns until healing.”

Here the perfume itself has a defined therapeutic power, and rose oil also serves as a medicinal vehicle in wound treatment.
Reference: Galen, *De simpl. med.* XI, Kühn 12.45.14.

5. Perfumes can act through smell.

Greek

> ἀλείφειν δὲ μύροις τοῖσδε χρὴ τὸ μέτωπον καὶ ῥινῶν τοὺς πόρους, μάλιστα μὲν ἰρίνῳ καὶ ἀμαρακίνῳ καὶ ναρδίνῳ…
> ἀξιοῖ διαχρίειν τοῖς τοιούτοις μύροις τὰς ῥῖνας, ὡς διὰ τῆς ὀσμῆς αὐτῶν θερμαινομένης τῆς κεφαλῆς.

Translation
“One should anoint the forehead and the pores of the nostrils with these perfumes, especially iris, amarakinon, and nard perfume… He recommends anointing the nostrils with such perfumes, so that the head is warmed by their smell.”

This is Galen’s clearest statement that perfumes can work medicinally through olfactory action.
Reference: Galen, *De compositione medicamentorum secundum locos* X, Kühn 12.511.17; 12.542.2.

6. Galen’s pharmaceutical quality control: “good” rose oil is not just long-lasting fragrance.

Greek

> μέμνησο δ' ὅτι καλὸν ἀεὶ λέγω ῥόδινον, ἐν ᾧ πλεῖστα μὲν ἀποβέβρεκται ῥόδα, τὸ δὲ ἔλαιον… ἔστω, εἰ οἷόν τε, χωρὶς ἁλῶν ἐσκευασμένον, οὐδενὸς ἄλλου προςεμβεβλημένου τοῖς ῥόδοις, καθάπερ οἱ τὰ στύμματα προςεμβάλλοντες ποιοῦσιν, ἕνεκα τοῦ πολλῷ χρόνῳ διαμεῖναι τὸ ῥόδινον εὐῶδες.

Translation
“Remember that by ‘good’ rose oil I always mean one in which as many roses as possible have been steeped, and let the oil, if possible, be of the sort called ōmotribes, prepared without salts and with nothing else added to the roses, unlike those who put in astringent ingredients so that the rose oil remains fragrant for a long time.”

This closely matches the full distinction passage: Galen resists perfumers’ preservative techniques when they distort the medicine.
Reference: Galen, *De comp. med. sec. loc.* X, Kühn 12.604.14.

7. Perfumes can even be administered internally.

Greek

> ἢ νάρδου μύρου κοχλιάρια δʹ. πίνειν δίδου καθ' ἡμέραν, καὶ μάλιστα ἐπὶ τῶν σκιῤῥουμένων.
> ἢ πήγανον λεάνας καὶ ἰρίνῳ μύρῳ μίξας δίδου πίνειν.

Translation
“Or give four spoonfuls of nard perfume to drink daily, especially in indurated cases.”
“Or grind rue, mix it with iris perfume, and give it to drink.”

This is an important corrective to any modern assumption that μύρα are merely topical fragrances. For Galen they can be internal drugs.
References: Galen, *De comp. med. sec. loc.* X, Kühn 13.217.18; *De antidotis* II, Kühn 14.141.15.

8. Galen marks a boundary: luxury perfumery lies outside medicine.

Greek

> καταπλάσματα τῶν ἀρωμάτων ἐν ἱματίοις, εὐώδεις τε βαφὰς ἱματίων καὶ ῥάσματα θαλάμων καὶ περιπάτων, θυμιαμάτων τε παντοίων ποικιλίας καὶ χρισμάτων καὶ μύρων, ἔξω γὰρ ταῦτα τῆς ἰατρικῆς.

Translation
“Perfume-poultices for garments, scented dyes for garments, strewing-materials for rooms and walks, varieties of incense-compounds, and ointments and perfumes: these lie outside medicine.”

This negative statement matters because it shows Galen policing the line between medical perfumery and luxury or cosmetic scenting.
Reference: Galen, *De comp. med. sec. loc.* X, Kühn 12.450.2.

Taken together, these passages support a compact argument: in Galen, μύρα are compounded aromatic medicaments. They are distinguished from plain oils, altered by added ingredients, assessed by bodily effect rather than smell, used topically and by inhalation, sometimes ingested, and sharply separated from non-medical perfumery.
